Local isomorphisms and birational geometry

A bijective algebraic map can fail to have an algebraic inverse. The parametrization \(t\mapsto(t^2,t^3)\) reaches every point of a cusp exactly once, but its inverse requires a function that is not regular at the cusp. Local rings detect the missing inverse that point sets and topology overlook.

Lecture spine: L35–38. The four recordings develop local pullbacks, the stalk criterion for an isomorphism, intersections of local rings, extension of a local-ring isomorphism to neighborhoods, and birationality through function fields. The proofs and cusp calculations below are independently written. All varieties are irreducible over the fixed algebraically closed field \(k\), and all algebra isomorphisms preserve \(k\).

14.1 Local pullbacks retain the marked point L35 05:00

Let \(f:X\to Y\) be a morphism, let \(p\in X\), and write \(q=f(p)\). A germ \([V,h]\in\mathcal O_{Y,q}\) is represented by a regular function \(h\) on an open neighborhood \(V\) of \(q\). Pulling it back gives the germ \([f^{-1}(V),h\circ f]\) at \(p\). Equality of germs is preserved because two functions equal near \(q\) pull back to functions equal near \(p\). Thus \(f\) induces a \(k\)-algebra homomorphism

\[ f^\#_p:\mathcal O_{Y,q}\longrightarrow\mathcal O_{X,p}. \]

The arrows reverse because functions on the target are evaluated after applying \(f\). This map is local: the inverse image of the maximal ideal \(\mathfrak m_p\) is \(\mathfrak m_q\). A germ lies in the maximal ideal exactly when its value at the marked point is zero, and \((h\circ f)(p)=h(q)\). Therefore the induced map on residue fields \(\mathcal O_{Y,q}/\mathfrak m_q\to\mathcal O_{X,p}/\mathfrak m_p\) is the identity on \(k\). This local condition records where the point goes.

In affine coordinates, if \(X\) has ring \(A\), \(Y\) has ring \(B\), and the pullback is \(\varphi:B\to A\), then \(f^\#_p\) is the map \(B_{\mathfrak m_q}\to A_{\mathfrak m_p}\) obtained by localizing \(\varphi\). A denominator nonzero at \(q\) pulls back to one nonzero at \(p\), so the fraction formula is defined.

For the cusp \(C=V(y^2-x^3)\), put \(B=k[t^2,t^3]\subset k[t]=A\). The parametrization \(\nu:\mathbb A^1\to C\) induces inclusion \(B\hookrightarrow A\). At the origins the local pullback is

\[ k[t^2,t^3]_{(t^2,t^3)}\hookrightarrow k[t]_{(t)}. \]

The element \(t\) belongs to the right ring and not the left. If \(t=a(t^2,t^3)/b(t^2,t^3)\) with \(b(0)\ne0\), then \(tb=a\). The left side has a nonzero coefficient of \(t\), while a polynomial in \(t^2,t^3\) has no term of degree one. This contradiction proves nonmembership. The lost first-order function is the algebraic obstruction to an inverse near the cusp. The interactive cusp figure visualizes the parametrization; its drawing complements this local algebra calculation.

14.2 A homeomorphism plus local algebra L35 15:00

Theorem 14.1. A morphism \(f:X\to Y\) is an isomorphism if and only if it is a homeomorphism and every induced map \(f^\#_p:\mathcal O_{Y,f(p)}\to\mathcal O_{X,p}\) is an isomorphism.

An isomorphism has a morphism inverse; pulling back in the two directions gives inverse maps of all local rings, and the underlying maps are inverse homeomorphisms. For the converse, the assumed homeomorphism has a continuous inverse \(g:Y\to X\). We need to prove that \(g\) pulls regular functions back to regular functions.

Let \(U\subseteq X\) be open and \(h\in\mathcal O_X(U)\). The set \(V=f(U)\) is open because \(f\) is a homeomorphism. Fix \(q\in V\) and \(p=g(q)\). Surjectivity of \(f^\#_p\) gives a germ \([W,a]\in\mathcal O_{Y,q}\) pulling back to the germ of \(h\) at \(p\). Shrink \(W\) inside \(V\). Equality of the two germs means that \(a\circ f=h\) on some neighborhood \(U'\) of \(p\) contained in \(U\cap f^{-1}(W)\). Since \(f\) is open, \(f(U')\) is a neighborhood of \(q\). There \(a=h\circ g\). Thus \(h\circ g\) is regular near every \(q\), hence regular on \(V\). This proves that \(g\) is a morphism and finishes the theorem. ∎

The proof explains why merely listing abstract isomorphisms between local rings is insufficient for this criterion. The isomorphisms must be the pullbacks induced by the particular morphism under consideration. They then compare the actual local functions appearing in that morphism.

The cusp map is a homeomorphism on the classical Zariski spaces but not an isomorphism. It is bijective: when \(x\ne0\), the parameter is \(t=y/x\); when \(x=0\), the equation forces \(y=0\). To check the topology directly, every proper closed subset of the cusp is contained in the zero locus of some nonzero element of \(B=k[t^2,t^3]\). Its pullback is a nonzero polynomial in \(t\), which has finitely many roots. Bijectivity therefore makes every proper closed subset of the cusp finite. Conversely finite sets are closed, since classical points are closed. The line and the cusp both have the cofinite topology, so the bijection is a homeomorphism. The non-surjective local pullback at zero shows exactly why the stronger algebraic conclusion fails.

14.3 A rational function regular at every point L36 00:00

All local rings of an irreducible variety embed in the common function field \(k(X)\). The embedding sends a germ to its rational-function class. It is injective because a regular function vanishing on a nonempty open subset of an irreducible variety vanishes on its entire domain. Thus an intersection of local rings has a definite meaning only after specifying this common ambient field.

Theorem 14.2. For every nonempty open \(U\subseteq X\), \[ \mathcal O_X(U)=\bigcap_{p\in U}\mathcal O_{X,p} \quad\text{inside }k(X). \]

A regular function on \(U\) gives a germ at every point, proving one inclusion. Conversely let \(r\in k(X)\) belong to every local ring on \(U\). At each \(p\), choose a regular function \(h_p\) on a neighborhood \(U_p\subseteq U\) representing that same element \(r\). On any nonempty overlap, the difference \(h_p-h_q\) is zero as a rational function. It is therefore zero on a dense open subset of the overlap, and then everywhere there. The functions agree and glue to a regular function on \(U\) representing \(r\). ∎

There is a useful algebraic version valid for any domain \(A\), with fraction field \(K\): \[ A=\bigcap_{\mathfrak m\in\operatorname{MaxSpec}A}A_{\mathfrak m}\subseteq K. \] To prove it, fix \(r\in K\) and form the denominator ideal \(J_r=\{a\in A:ar\in A\}\). If \(r\in A_{\mathfrak m}\), some denominator \(s\notin\mathfrak m\) has \(sr\in A\); hence \(J_r\not\subseteq\mathfrak m\). If this holds for all maximal ideals, \(J_r\) cannot be proper, because every proper ideal is contained in a maximal ideal. Thus \(1\in J_r\), so \(r\in A\).

For \(X=\mathbb P^1\) with coordinate \(t\) on one affine chart, \(t\) belongs to every finite-point local ring but not the local ring at infinity. There the coordinate is \(s=1/t\), and \(t=1/s\) has a pole. Intersecting all local rings therefore eliminates nonconstant polynomials as well as other rational functions and leaves only \(k\). No one affine chart can detect this global restriction by itself.

14.4 Extending a local-ring isomorphism L37 05:00

Theorem 14.3. Let \(p\in X\), \(q\in Y\). A \(k\)-algebra isomorphism \(\alpha:\mathcal O_{Y,q}\to\mathcal O_{X,p}\) is induced by an isomorphism \(f:U\to V\) between open neighborhoods of \(p\) and \(q\), with \(f(p)=q\).

Choose affine neighborhoods \(X_0,Y_0\) of the marked points, with coordinate domains \(A,B\). Their local rings are \(A_{\mathfrak m_p}\) and \(B_{\mathfrak m_q}\). Choose finite algebra generators \(b_1,\ldots,b_r\) of \(B\). Each \(\alpha(b_i)\) is a fraction in \(A\) with denominator nonzero at \(p\). Intersecting their finitely many denominator opens gives an open neighborhood \(U_0\subseteq X_0\) where all these functions are regular.

The relations among the \(b_i\) remain true under \(\alpha\). They hold as rational-function identities, and therefore as regular identities on \(U_0\). The affine-target correspondence of Chapter 8 gives a morphism \(f:U_0\to Y_0\). Since an isomorphism of local rings preserves maximal ideals, and \(\alpha\) fixes the residue field \(k\), evaluation shows \(f(p)=q\). Repeating the construction for \(\alpha^{-1}\), using finite generators of \(A\), gives \(g:V_0\to X_0\) with \(g(q)=p\).

We must shrink once more; the two initial maps need not land in one another's initial domains. Restrict \(f\) to \(f^{-1}(V_0)\) and \(g\) to \(g^{-1}(U_0)\). Their composites have the identity germ at the marked points, since their pullbacks induce \(\alpha\alpha^{-1}\) and \(\alpha^{-1}\alpha\). Checking the finitely many affine coordinate functions shows each composite equals the identity on a sufficiently small open neighborhood. Choose such a neighborhood \(V\) of \(q\) where \(f\circ g\) is the identity and whose image under \(g\) lies in the neighborhood where \(g\circ f\) is the identity. Put \(U=f^{-1}(V)\) inside that latter neighborhood. Then \(f:U\to V\) and \(g:V\to U\) are inverse morphisms, and the original local pullback is \(\alpha\). ∎

Finite generation is the crucial step: only finitely many coordinate denominators must be made nonzero simultaneously. The theorem concerns local \(k\)-algebras, not completed local rings. Two completions being isomorphic gives formal local information and does not, by this argument, produce a Zariski-neighborhood isomorphism. Nor does the theorem assert that \(X\) and \(Y\) themselves are globally isomorphic.

For example, the local ring of \(\mathbb A^1\) at 1 equals the local ring of the punctured line \(\mathbb A^1\setminus\{0\}\) at 1. These varieties share a neighborhood of 1 but are not isomorphic globally: the punctured line has the nonconstant unit \(t\), while all units of \(k[t]\) are constants.

14.5 Function fields classify birational geometry L38 25:00

A rational map \(X\dashrightarrow Y\) is an equivalence class of morphisms \(U\to Y\) on nonempty open subsets \(U\subseteq X\). Two representatives agree if their restrictions coincide on a nonempty open subset of their common domain. For the irreducible sources and separated varieties in this course this extends the dominant-morphism discussion in Chapter 12. A representative is dominant when its image is dense in the target.

Definition 14.4. Irreducible varieties \(X,Y\) are birational if nonempty open subsets \(U\subseteq X\), \(V\subseteq Y\) are isomorphic. A birational morphism is an everywhere-defined morphism whose restriction is an isomorphism between suitable dense open subsets of source and target.

Birationality is an equivalence relation. Reflexivity and symmetry come from identity and inverse maps. For transitivity, two nonempty open subsets used in the middle variety intersect because that variety is irreducible. Restrict both isomorphisms to the intersection and compose. This is where irreducibility makes the composition nonempty.

Theorem 14.5. The varieties \(X,Y\) are birational over \(k\) if and only if \(k(X)\) and \(k(Y)\) are isomorphic as extensions of \(k\).

An isomorphism between nonempty opens identifies their rational functions; passing to a smaller nonempty open does not change a function field. This gives the forward implication. Conversely let \(\beta:k(Y)\to k(X)\) be a \(k\)-isomorphism. Choose affine opens with coordinate domains \(A,B\), and finite generators \(b_1,\ldots,b_r\) of \(B\). Represent each \(\beta(b_i)\) as a fraction in \(A\). Their nonzero denominators define a common nonempty open \(U_0\subseteq X\), and the algebraic relations among the \(b_i\) give a morphism \(f:U_0\to Y\). Its pullback on function fields is \(\beta\), so it is dominant. Indeed a proper closed image closure in an affine target would have a nonzero vanishing function whose pullback is zero, contradicting injectivity.

Applying the same construction to \(\beta^{-1}\) gives a dominant rational inverse \(g\). Dominance ensures that the preimages of the needed nonempty open domains are nonempty. On common domains the composites induce identity maps on function fields. Their coordinate functions therefore agree with those of the identity, so the composites are identity morphisms there. Shrinking domains as in Theorem 14.3 gives inverse isomorphisms between nonempty opens. ∎

The correspondence between dominant rational maps and embeddings of function fields is the more general categorical result; Stacks, Theorem 33.4.1 states it for varieties over an arbitrary field. Here the proof is expressed in affine generators so that the denominator mechanism remains visible.

The cusp and the line have the same function field \(k(t)\), because \(t=t^3/t^2=y/x\) on the cusp away from its origin. They are birational even though their marked origin local rings are not isomorphic. Thus the lecture title connecting local rings, function fields, and birationality must be read with its quantifiers: one local-ring isomorphism implies birationality; birationality supplies isomorphic local rings on corresponding points of suitable open subsets. It does not identify the local rings at arbitrary prescribed points.

14.6 Exercises and complete solutions

  1. Prove that a \(k\)-isomorphism of the local rings at two closed points induces the identity on their residue fields. Where is the fixed \(k\)-structure used?
  2. For \(A=k[t^2,t^3]\) and its origin maximal ideal \(\mathfrak m\), prove \(t\notin A_{\mathfrak m}\) but \(t\in A_{t^2}\).
  3. Determine whether \(\mathbb A^1\) and \(\mathbb P^1\) are isomorphic, birational, both, or neither.
  4. Prove the intersection identity for \(A=k[t]\) directly using a reduced fraction \(a(t)/b(t)\).
  5. Why does a birational morphism need not induce isomorphisms on every local ring? Give a specific point and missing germ.
  6. Explain why the local-ring neighborhood theorem cannot be applied by replacing a local ring with its residue field.
Worked solutions

1. A ring isomorphism preserves units. In a local ring the nonunits form its unique maximal ideal, so the isomorphism sends one maximal ideal onto the other. It therefore induces an isomorphism of the quotients. At a closed point of a variety over algebraically closed \(k\), the residue field is canonically \(k\), by evaluation and the Nullstellensatz. A \(k\)-algebra map fixes each constant, so the induced map is the identity under these identifications. An abstract ring isomorphism could instead act through a nontrivial automorphism of \(k\).

2. The coefficient-of-\(t\) argument from Section 14.1 proves the first statement: a denominator with nonzero constant term cannot make \(t\) equal a fraction of cusp polynomials. For the second, \(t=t^3/t^2\), and both numerator and denominator belong to \(A\), with the denominator inverted in \(A_{t^2}\). The obstruction is concentrated at the origin, which is absent from that principal open.

3. They are birational because the standard chart \(\mathbb P^1\setminus\{\infty\}\) is \(\mathbb A^1\). They are not isomorphic: \(\mathbb A^1\) has the nonconstant global regular function \(t\), whereas \(\mathbb P^1\) has only constants. An isomorphism would give an isomorphism of global function rings preserving \(k\). Removing a closed point changes global geometry without changing the function field.

4. Cancel the common factors of \(a,b\). If \(b\) is nonconstant, it has a root \(c\in k\), and coprimality gives \(a(c)\ne0\). Membership in \(k[t]_{(t-c)}\) would give \(a/b=u/v\) with \(v(c)\ne0\). Cross multiplication yields \(a(c)v(c)=b(c)u(c)=0\), a contradiction. Thus membership in every closed-point local ring forces \(b\) constant and the fraction polynomial. Every polynomial belongs to every localization, proving equality.

5. Use the cusp parametrization \(\nu\). Its function-field pullback is an isomorphism and it restricts to an isomorphism away from zero. At the origin the local pullback lacks the germ \(t\) on the source. Birationality controls a dense open subset, while the singular exceptional point can retain different local algebra.

6. Every closed-point residue field in this course is \(k\). A smooth line point and a cusp point therefore have isomorphic residue fields, even though their local rings have different first-order structure. The residue field remembers values at the point; the local ring also remembers germs on neighborhoods. The theorem requires all that local algebra, not only its quotient by the maximal ideal.

Boundary: the cusp homeomorphism is checked directly through the cofinite topology, without invoking a general finite-morphism theorem. Neighborhood extension uses finite-type coordinate algebras and genuine local \(k\)-algebra isomorphisms. Birational equivalence forgets the complement of suitable dense opens; it does not certify global isomorphism or preservation of a chosen singular point.
