A polynomial may factor into several pieces; an algebraic set likewise decomposes into finitely many irreducible components. The finiteness does not come from a picture of curves. It comes from the ascending-chain condition on ideals, translated into a descending-chain condition on closed sets.

Lecture spine: L6–7. This chapter includes an explicit correction to the real-hyperbola discussion: Euclidean disconnectedness does not imply Zariski disconnectedness. The original caption sequence is retained as a topic guide, not treated as an infallible mathematical authority.

3.1 The affine line and the topology trap L6 00:00

The ring \(k[t]\) is a principal ideal domain: it is a domain and every ideal is generated by one element. Division with remainder proves this by choosing a nonzero polynomial of minimum degree in a nonzero ideal. Every other polynomial in that ideal has zero remainder on division by the chosen one. Consequently every proper closed subset of \(\mathbb A^1\) is the zero locus of one nonzero polynomial and is finite. Conversely any finite set is cut out by the product of its distinct linear factors.

Thus the Zariski topology on the affine line is the cofinite topology: its nonempty open sets have finite complements. The whole line is irreducible because it is infinite. An algebraically closed field cannot be finite: if its elements were \(a_1,\ldots,a_r\), the nonconstant polynomial \(1+\prod_i(t-a_i)\) would have no root in the field.

Every closed point is a singleton, but distinct points cannot be separated by disjoint nonempty open neighborhoods. In particular, \(T_1\) does not imply Hausdorff. The later algebraic condition of separatedness concerns the diagonal of a variety in its algebraic product, not arbitrary Hausdorff separation in the ordinary product topology. The distinction becomes concrete in Chapter 9.

Correction to the L6 discussion near 25:00. The real hyperbola \(xy=1\) has two Euclidean connected components, but it is irreducible in its real Zariski topology. Its coordinate ring is \(\mathbb R[t,t^{-1}]\). A nonzero Laurent polynomial has only finitely many roots in \(\mathbb R^\times\), after multiplication by a power of \(t\). Hence every proper real Zariski closed subset of the hyperbola is finite, while the hyperbola is infinite. It cannot be a union of two proper closed subsets. Algebraic closure is essential to the general point–ideal dictionary, but this hyperbola is not a counterexample to Zariski irreducibility over \(\mathbb R\).

The genuine failure over \(\mathbb R\) is already \(x^2+1\): its ideal is proper and prime, yet its real zero locus is empty. Whenever a statement concerns connectedness, closures, or components, the topology must be named. A drawing of real points usually shows Euclidean geometry, not all the information in the Zariski topology over \(k\).

3.2 The descending-chain condition L6 30:00

Definition 3.1. A topological space \(X\) is Noetherian if every descending chain \(F_1\supseteq F_2\supseteq\cdots\) of closed subsets eventually stabilizes. Equivalently, every ascending chain of open subsets stabilizes.

Affine space is Noetherian. If \(F_i\) is a descending chain of algebraic sets, then \(I(F_i)\) is an ascending chain of ideals in the Noetherian polynomial ring. It stabilizes by the Hilbert basis theorem; applying \(V\) recovers stabilization of the closed sets. Every closed algebraic subset inherits this property.

Proposition 3.2. Every subspace of a Noetherian space is Noetherian.

Let \(Y\subseteq X\), and write a descending chain of relatively closed subsets as \(E_i=Y\cap F_i\), where \(F_i\) is closed in \(X\). The chosen \(F_i\) need not descend. Replace it by \(G_i=F_1\cap\cdots\cap F_i\). Then \(G_i\) descends, and \(Y\cap G_i=E_i\), since the \(E_i\) already descend. Stabilization of \(G_i\) implies stabilization of \(E_i\). ∎

The chain condition also says that every nonempty collection of closed subsets has a minimal member under inclusion. Otherwise, choose a member, then a strictly smaller one, and continue to obtain an infinite strictly descending chain. This minimal member need not be unique and need not be contained in every other member. “Minimal” is weaker than “smallest.” The minimal-counterexample method below needs only minimality.

Noetherian does not mean finite as a set. The affine line has infinitely many points and is Noetherian. Nor does the chain condition alone supply a uniform finite bound on the lengths of all chains. For finite-type varieties, dimension will provide a separate numerical invariant; it should not be built into the definition of Noetherianity.

3.3 Existence of finitely many components L7 25:00

Theorem 3.3. Every nonempty closed subset \(Y\) of a Noetherian space is a finite union of irreducible closed subsets. After removing any member contained in another, the remaining subsets are unique up to order. They are the irreducible components of \(Y\).

First prove existence. Suppose some closed subsets admit no finite decomposition, and choose a minimal such subset \(Y\). It cannot be irreducible, since a single irreducible subset is already a finite decomposition. Write \(Y=F\cup G\) with \(F,G\) proper closed subsets of \(Y\), hence closed in the ambient space. Minimality implies that both admit finite irreducible decompositions. Joining those decompositions gives one for \(Y\), a contradiction.

Remove redundant members from any resulting finite decomposition. For uniqueness suppose \(Y=Y_1\cup\cdots\cup Y_r=Z_1\cup\cdots\cup Z_s\), with all members irreducible, closed, and no member contained in another of its own list. Irreducibility implies that \(Y_i\), being covered by the relatively closed sets \(Y_i\cap Z_j\), is contained in some \(Z_j\). Applying the same argument to \(Z_j\) puts it inside some \(Y_\ell\). Then \(Y_i\subseteq Y_\ell\), so irredundancy forces \(i=\ell\). Both inclusions are therefore equalities, and \(Y_i=Z_j\). Applying this to all members of both lists proves they are the same list up to order. ∎

No set-theoretic cancellation of unions is needed in this proof. In general \(A\cup B=A\cup C\) does not imply \(B=C\). The irreducibility and noncontainment arguments are the steps that make uniqueness work.

For an algebraic set \(Y\subseteq\mathbb A^n\), each component \(Y_i\) corresponds to a prime ideal \(P_i=I(Y_i)\), and \[ I(Y)=\bigcap_{i=1}^r P_i. \] Indeed a polynomial vanishes on the union precisely when it vanishes on every component. These primes are exactly the prime ideals minimal among those containing \(I(Y)\). If a prime \(P\) contains \(I(Y)\), its irreducible zero locus lies in the finite union of the \(Y_i\), so it lies in one \(Y_i\); order reversal gives \(P_i\subseteq P\). Minimality then identifies the minimal primes.

3.4 Components are not connected components

The set \(V(xy(x-1))\subseteq\mathbb A^2\) has three irreducible components: the two vertical lines \(x=0,x=1\), and the horizontal line \(y=0\). The horizontal line meets both vertical lines, so their union is connected. The three prime ideals are \((x),(x-1),(y)\). Their intersection is the principal ideal \((xy(x-1))\): divisibility by each distinct prime factor implies divisibility by their product in the unique factorization domain \(k[x,y]\).

The distinction can run in the other direction: the two parallel lines \(V(x(x-1))\) are disconnected. Each line is closed and is the complement of the other inside the union, hence also open there. A connected component is maximal for connectedness, while an irreducible component is maximal for irreducibility. Connected components can contain several irreducible components.

The laboratory factors the displayed polynomial and checks the component equations exactly. A plot may help visualize the incidences, but factorization and the prime-ideal argument establish the decomposition over the specified field.

3.5 Exercises and complete solutions

  1. Show that a Noetherian Hausdorff space is finite.
  2. Find an irredundant decomposition of \(V(xy,xz)\subseteq\mathbb A^3\).
  3. For the decomposition in Exercise 2, compute the closure of the complement of the plane component.
  4. Explain why a nonempty collection of closed sets having a minimal element does not require that element to be a point.
Worked solutions

1. Apply Theorem 3.3 to the whole space. Each irreducible closed component of a Hausdorff space has at most one point, by Chapter 2. There are finitely many components, so there are finitely many points. In particular, the usual Euclidean topology on an infinite interval is not Noetherian.

2. At a common zero, either \(x=0\), or \(x\ne0\) forces \(y=z=0\). Thus the set is \(V(x)\cup V(y,z)\), a plane and the \(x\)-axis. Their coordinate rings are \(k[y,z]\) and \(k[x]\), domains, so both are irreducible. Neither contains the other. The full radical ideal is \((x)\cap(y,z)=(xy,xz)\): an element divisible by \(x\) and in \((y,z)\) is \(x\) times an element of \((y,z)\), because reduction modulo \((y,z)\) lands in \(k[x]\), a domain.

3. Removing \(V(x)\) leaves the \(x\)-axis with its origin removed. This is a nonempty open subset of the irreducible axis, so its closure is the whole axis \(V(y,z)\). It does not recover the plane. Removing a component and then taking closure recovers the other components when the decomposition is irredundant.

4. Minimality is relative to the chosen collection. The collection could consist only of planes, or contain only the whole space. A minimal member then need not be minimal among all nonempty closed subsets. The decomposition proof chooses its minimal member among the closed subsets that fail a proposed property.

Boundary: the Noetherian decomposition is a theorem about closed sets and contains no multiplicity data. It is not a primary decomposition of a nonradical ideal. The ordinary real hyperbola correction distinguishes Euclidean and Zariski topology rather than weakening either definition.
