Recovering affine geometry from its ring

The affine plane can sit in itself, in three-space as \(z=0\), or as the graph \(z=xy\). Its coordinates and equations change, but its polynomial functions form isomorphic \(k\)-algebras. Recovering a variety from its ring of functions separates geometry from an arbitrary embedding.

Lecture spine: L11–12: coordinate rings, prime and maximal spectra, the topology on spectra, quotients, and localization. The distinction between a recovered topological space and a recovered algebraic variety is retained throughout.

5.1 Different presentations of the same functions L11 10:00

The plane \(z=0\) has ring \(k[x,y,z]/(z)\cong k[x,y]\); the graph \(z=xy\) has ring \(k[x,y,z]/(z-xy)\cong k[x,y]\). Restricting a polynomial to either surface amounts to replacing \(z\) by its defining expression. The ring isomorphisms are constructive, not a consequence merely of both spaces having dimension two.

A \(k\)-algebra \(A\) is finitely generated if finitely many elements \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) generate it using sums, products, and scalar multiplication. Such a choice gives a surjection \(k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\to A\), with \(x_i\mapsto a_i\). If \(A\) is a domain, the kernel is prime. The algebra therefore presents an affine variety \(V(\ker)\), whose coordinate ring is \(A\) by the Nullstellensatz. Different generating sets can produce different embeddings of the same abstract affine variety.

For example, \(k[t]\) can be generated by \(t\), presenting the line, or by \(t,t^2\), presenting the parabola. This does not say that any domain of transcendence degree one is \(k[t]\). The punctured line has ring \(k[t,t^{-1}]\), and the cusp has ring \(k[t^2,t^3]\). They share a fraction field with the line but not its entire coordinate ring. Functions regular everywhere retain information that rational functions forget.

5.2 Building a space from prime ideals L11 30:00

Definition 5.1. The prime spectrum \(\operatorname{Spec}A\) is the set of prime ideals of a ring \(A\). For an ideal \(J\), put \(V_A(J)=\{P\in\operatorname{Spec}A:J\subseteq P\}\). These are the closed sets of its Zariski topology. The maximal spectrum \(\operatorname{MaxSpec}A\) is the subspace consisting of maximal ideals.

The spectrum identities follow from primality: \(V_A(IJ)=V_A(I)\cup V_A(J)\), and intersections correspond to sums of ideals. This construction works for arbitrary commutative rings, without an algebraically closed field and without ordinary coordinate points. It builds the candidate geometric space out of algebra itself.

The analogy has a precise limit. \(\operatorname{Spec}k[t]\) contains the ideals \((t-a)\), which represent classical points, and also the prime \((0)\). The closure of a prime \(P\), regarded as a point of the spectrum, is \(V_A(P)\), since these are exactly the primes containing \(P\). Thus \((0)\) is dense when \(A\) is a domain. It is called a generic point. There is no dense singleton among the ordinary closed points of the affine line.

Every irreducible closed subset of a spectrum has a generic point. Here the input is the general algebra fact that an ideal's radical is the intersection of all primes containing it, as in Stacks, Lemma 10.17.2(7). To see its consequence, write a nonempty irreducible closed set as \(V_A(J)\) with \(J\) radical. If \(ab\in J\), then \(V_A(J)\subseteq V_A(a)\cup V_A(b)\); irreducibility puts it inside one of those closed sets. The intersection-of-primes fact implies \(a\in J\) or \(b\in J\). Thus \(J\) is prime, and the already computed closure of the point \(J\) is \(V_A(J)\). This is also Stacks, Lemma 10.26.1.

Ordinary classical varieties are usually recovered from maximal ideals; schemes retain all the primes and a sheaf of functions. A topological spectrum alone does not contain that sheaf. For example, a field and its ring of dual numbers \(k[\epsilon]/(\epsilon^2)\) both have a one-point spectrum, but the second ring contains a nonzero nilpotent.

5.3 Quotients and reversal of arrows L12 00:00

A ring map \(\varphi:A\to B\) induces a map \(\varphi^\sharp:\operatorname{Spec}B\to\operatorname{Spec}A\) by inverse image \(Q\mapsto\varphi^{-1}(Q)\). The inverse image is prime: if \(\varphi(ab)\in Q\), then \(\varphi(a)\varphi(b)\in Q\), so one factor is in \(Q\). The inverse image is proper because the map preserves 1. Continuity follows from

\[ (\varphi^\sharp)^{-1}(V_A(J))=V_B(\varphi(J)B). \]

The arrows reverse direction because functions are pulled back from the target to the source. Composition reverses in the corresponding way. A construction respecting identities and composition is a functor; it is contravariant when it reverses arrows. No additional categorical machinery is needed to check these identities.

Proposition 5.2. The spectrum of \(A/J\) is naturally homeomorphic to \(V_A(J)\).

Under the quotient map, prime ideals in \(A/J\) correspond exactly to prime ideals of \(A\) containing \(J\); a prime \(P\) corresponds to \(P/J\). This is a bijection onto \(V_A(J)\). A closed subset defined by an ideal of \(A/J\) corresponds to a closed subset \(V_A(I)\) with \(J\subseteq I\). Hence both the map and its inverse preserve closed subsets, proving the homeomorphism. ∎

Thus imposing equations means taking a quotient of the ring. Inverting a function instead restricts to a basic open subset, as developed in Chapter 6. These two operations move geometry in different directions: quotienting removes points where new equations fail; localization removes points where a denominator vanishes.

5.4 Recovering the classical topology L12 10:00

Proposition 5.3. For an affine algebraic set \(X\) over \(k\), the map \(p\mapsto\mathfrak m_p\) is a homeomorphism \(X\to\operatorname{MaxSpec}k[X]\).

The Nullstellensatz gives bijectivity. A closed subset of \(X\) is cut out by functions \(f\) in some ideal \(J\subseteq k[X]\). A point \(p\) belongs to it exactly when each \(f\in J\) vanishes at \(p\), which means \(J\subseteq\mathfrak m_p\). This is precisely membership in \(V_{k[X]}(J)\cap\operatorname{MaxSpec}k[X]\). The correspondence therefore identifies the closed sets on both sides. ∎

It follows that a \(k\)-algebra isomorphism between coordinate rings induces a homeomorphism of the classical spaces. The stronger assertion that it induces an isomorphism of varieties needs regular functions and morphisms, not just topology. Conversely, a homeomorphism of classical spaces need not arise from any algebraic map. On an affine line, every permutation preserves the cofinite topology, but most permutations are not polynomial.

This is the first step toward the full affine dictionary: a finitely generated reduced \(k\)-algebra remembers an affine algebraic set, and a finitely generated domain remembers an affine variety. Keeping the \(k\)-algebra structure matters. A field automorphism acting on coefficients is a ring map but need not be a morphism over the fixed field \(k\).

5.5 Exercises and complete solutions

  1. Describe \(\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb Z\) and the closure of each of its points.
  2. Find the map on spectra induced by \(k[u]\to k[t]\), \(u\mapsto t^2\).
  3. Explain why \(k[t]\) and \(k[t,t^{-1}]\) are not isomorphic as \(k\)-algebras.
Worked solutions

1. The prime ideals are \((0)\) and \((p)\) for positive prime integers \(p\). Each \((p)\) is maximal and has singleton closure. The closure of \((0)\) is the whole spectrum because every prime contains zero. This is a ring-built space; its points are not integer solutions of an equation in an ambient affine space over \(k\).

2. A closed point \(t=a\) maps to \(u=a^2\), since the inverse image of \((t-a)\) consists of polynomials in \(u\) vanishing at \(a^2\). The generic point maps to the generic point, since the substitution is injective. In characteristic different from two, a nonzero closed point of the target has two preimages over an algebraically closed field; zero has one. In characteristic two this fiber count changes.

3. Units of \(k[t]\) are exactly \(k^\times\), by degree of a product. The Laurent ring has the nonconstant unit \(t\). If a \(k\)-algebra isomorphism from the Laurent ring to \(k[t]\) existed, \(t\) would map to a scalar \(c\); then the nonzero element \(t-c\) would lie in its kernel. This contradicts injectivity. An isomorphism must preserve multiplication and invertibility, not just dimension or cardinality.
