Basic open sets and localization

The function \(1/x\) is algebraic and harmless on a punctured line, although it cannot be a polynomial on the whole line. Removing a zero locus should enlarge the available functions by permitting denominators. Localization makes that statement exact.

Lecture spine: L13–14. Principal open subsets are studied first, followed by their rings and affine realizations. The theorem that every locally regular function on a principal open comes from the localization is proved in Chapter 7 after regular functions are defined.

6.1 Nonvanishing loci form a basis L13 10:00

Let \(X\) be an affine variety and \(A=k[X]\). For \(f\in A\), put \(D_X(f)=\{p\in X:f(p)\ne0\}\), often shortened to \(D(f)\). Such a subset is called a principal or basic open. Its complement is the zero locus of \(f\) in \(X\). The identities \(D(1)=X\), \(D(0)=\varnothing\), and \(D(f)\cap D(g)=D(fg)\) follow by evaluation in the field \(k\).

If \(U\subseteq X\) is open, write its closed complement as \(V_X(J)\). The Noetherian ring \(A\) gives generators \(J=(f_1,\ldots,f_r)\); De Morgan's law then gives \(U=\bigcup_iD(f_i)\). Every open subset is a finite union of principal opens. This does not mean every open subset is itself principal, nor that it is affine.

The punctured plane \(U=\mathbb A^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}\) equals \(D(x)\cup D(y)\). It is not one principal open: if it were \(D(f)\), the zero locus \(V(f)\) would be precisely the origin, but a nonconstant polynomial in two variables has hypersurface components of dimension one. A nonzero constant has no zeros, and the zero polynomial vanishes everywhere. Chapter 8 will prove the stronger statement that the punctured plane is not affine.

6.2 Adjoining the reciprocal of a function L13 20:00

Definition 6.1. The localization \(A_f\) permits fractions \(a/f^m\), \(a\in A\), \(m\ge0\). In a general ring, \(a/f^m=b/f^n\) means that \(f^\ell(f^na-f^mb)=0\) for some \(\ell\ge0\). For a domain and \(f\ne0\), this reduces to ordinary cross multiplication.

For a point \(p\in D(f)\), evaluate \(a/f^m\) as \(a(p)/f(p)^m\). The equivalence relation guarantees that this is well-defined. If \(f\ne0\) in the coordinate domain, \(D(f)\) is a dense nonempty open in the irreducible \(X\). Two such fractions agreeing everywhere on \(D(f)\) are then equal in the fraction field: the cross-multiplied numerator vanishes on a dense subset and hence on all of \(X\). The exceptional case \(f=0\) gives an empty open set and the zero localization ring, not a subring of the fraction field.

Localization has a universal property. If a ring map \(\varphi:A\to B\) makes \(\varphi(f)\) invertible, there is a unique extension \(A_f\to B\), necessarily \(a/f^m\mapsto\varphi(a)\varphi(f)^{-m}\). The formula respects the equivalence relation, which proves existence; the images of \(A\) and \(1/f\) force uniqueness. This property is often easier to use than manipulating equivalence classes.

For \(A=k[x]\) and \(f=x\), the resulting ring is \(k[x,x^{-1}]\), whose elements are finite Laurent polynomials \(\sum_{j=-r}^s a_jx^j\). It does not include \(1/(x-1)\), because that function has a pole at the point \(x=1\), which remains in \(D(x)\). An allowed denominator is not an arbitrary nonzero polynomial: it must be invertible on the particular open set.

6.3 An open set that is an affine variety L14 00:00

Proposition 6.2. For \(f\ne0\), the principal open \(D_X(f)\) is isomorphic to the closed affine variety \[ Y=\{(p,z)\in X\times\mathbb A^1:f(p)z=1\}, \qquad k[Y]\cong A[z]/(fz-1)\cong A_f. \]

The projection \((p,z)\mapsto p\) lands in \(D(f)\). Its inverse is \(p\mapsto(p,1/f(p))\). Substituting either point map into a local polynomial fraction again gives a local polynomial fraction; a denominator stays nonzero on the inverse image of its nonvanishing locus. The two inverse point maps therefore preserve the local functions that Chapter 7 calls regular, giving the isomorphism criterion formalized in Chapter 8. This verification uses the explicit formulas rather than an abstract affine duality theorem. For the ring assertion, send \(z\) to \(1/f\). Conversely \(f\) becomes invertible in the quotient because \(fz=1\), so the universal property gives \(A_f\to A[z]/(fz-1)\). Both composites fix \(A\) and the additional reciprocal, hence are identities. Since \(A_f\) is a domain, the defining ideal is prime. ∎

Being open in one ambient space and closed in another causes no contradiction. A punctured line is open in \(\mathbb A^1\) and isomorphic to the closed hyperbola \(xz=1\) in \(\mathbb A^2\). Affineness is an intrinsic property, not a statement that a particular inclusion is closed.

On the hyperbola, projection to the \(x\)-axis misses zero because \(xz=1\). Its inverse recovers \(z=1/x\). The extra coordinate records the reciprocal rather than adding an independent degree of freedom. Algebraically, the relation \(xz=1\) removes the extra dimension, leaving a one-dimensional ring of Laurent polynomials.

6.4 Localization on spectra and overlaps

For any ring \(A\), prime ideals in \(A_f\) correspond to primes \(P\subseteq A\) avoiding \(f\). Extension sends \(P\) to \(PA_f\), and contraction recovers \(P\). If \(f\in P\), making \(f\) a unit would make the extended ideal the whole ring, so such a prime disappears. This gives a homeomorphism \(\operatorname{Spec}A_f\cong D(f)\) on spectra, with the same closed-set check used for quotients.

On an overlap \(D(f)\cap D(g)=D(fg)\), a function in \(A_f\) restricts by the natural map \(A_f\to A_{fg}\). Both \(f\) and \(g\) become invertible there. The ring maps from the two pieces into the common overlap are the algebraic data needed to compare and glue functions. For a domain they can all be viewed as inclusions into \(\operatorname{Frac}A\).

A nonempty principal open has the same dimension as \(X\). Its coordinate ring \(A_f\) has the same fraction field as \(A\), so the finite-type dimension theorem gives the equality. This proof applies to these nonempty opens of varieties; it does not assert that an arbitrary dense subset of an arbitrary topological space has the dimension of its closure.

6.5 Exercises and complete solutions

  1. Describe the coordinate ring of the affine line with the points 0 and 1 removed.
  2. Show that \(D(x^2)=D(x)\), and explain the resulting isomorphism of localizations.
  3. Compute \(A_x\) for \(A=k[x,y]/(xy)\). What geometric component survives?
  4. Why does the equivalence relation for localization need its extra power in a ring with zero divisors?
Worked solutions

1. The open is \(D(x(x-1))\), with ring \(k[x,1/(x(x-1))]\). This is also \(k[x,x^{-1},(x-1)^{-1}]\), because \(1/x=(x-1)/(x(x-1))\) and \(1/(x-1)=x/(x(x-1))\). Conversely the reciprocal of the product is the product of the two reciprocals.

2. A field element has nonzero square exactly when it is nonzero. In \(A_{x^2}\), \(1/x=x/x^2\), so inverting \(x^2\) also inverts \(x\). In \(A_x\), \(1/x^2\) already exists. The universal properties give mutually inverse maps.

3. Inverting \(x\) in the relation \(xy=0\) forces \(y=0\). The ring becomes \(k[x,x^{-1}]\). Geometrically only the \(x\)-axis with its origin removed remains; the whole \(y\)-axis lies in \(x=0\) and disappears.

4. In the same ring \(A\), the nonzero element \(y\) becomes zero in \(A_x\), since \(xy=0\) and \(x\) is invertible. Thus \(y/1=0/1\) in the localization even though \(y\ne0\) in \(A\). Equality must allow a power of the inverted element to annihilate the cross difference. Ordinary cross multiplication without this allowance would fail to construct the correct ring.
