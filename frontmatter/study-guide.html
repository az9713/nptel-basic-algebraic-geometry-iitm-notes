Routes, prerequisites, and retrieval practice

Study guide

The course's central skill is translation. Given a geometric assertion, identify the ring, ideal, localization, or field statement that makes it precise. Then check whether the reverse translation preserves the information you need.

Prerequisites to refresh

Before Chapter 1, be able to construct a quotient ring and prove that the kernel of a ring map is an ideal. Know that an ideal is prime exactly when its quotient is a domain, and maximal exactly when its quotient is a field. Polynomial division in one variable and factorization in polynomial rings supply the first examples.

For topology, know open and closed sets, induced topology, continuity, closure, and finite-subcover compactness. The Zariski topology behaves differently from Euclidean topology: large dense opens intersect, while classical points are still closed. Avoid importing a picture's Euclidean components into a statement about Zariski irreducibility.

Before function fields and nonsingularity, refresh algebraic independence, fraction fields, dual vector spaces, matrix rank, and formal partial derivatives. A derivative in a polynomial ring uses algebra, not limits; its behavior in positive characteristic must be checked separately.

A cumulative sequence of problems

  1. Parabola. Find its vanishing ideal and coordinate ring, prove irreducibility, construct the isomorphism with a line, and compute its local ring at a chosen point.
  2. Crossing. Decompose \(xy=0\), identify zero divisors, and explain why connectedness fails to isolate the irreducible pieces.
  3. Punctured line and plane. Compute their rings of regular functions. Explain why the first is affine while the second is not. Identify the exact place where the affine duality theorem is used.
  4. Cusp. Use \(x=t^2,y=t^3\). Recover the rational inverse on a dense open, prove the map is not an isomorphism at the origin, and compare the curve's dimension with its tangent-space dimension.
  5. Projective line. Glue its two affine charts; compute its function field, global regular functions, and local rings at a finite point and at infinity. Then compare the usual embedding with its Veronese conic.
  6. Node. Use \(x=t^2-1,y=t(t^2-1)\). Find the two parameter values mapping to the singular point and compare the tangent cone with the Zariski tangent space.

Each construction appears with worked reasoning in the chapters. The laboratory verifies selected identities; the curve explorer exposes the cusp/node contrast. Reconstruct the proofs before using either as a shortcut.

Where to go after the course

A natural next project is to implement a small dictionary explorer: enter polynomial generators, compute a Gröbner basis, inspect principal opens, and compare tangent ranks at selected points. Require it to state the field and distinguish ideal membership from radical membership. The most useful failure cases are often more educational than another smooth example.

For the next mathematical layer, learn affine schemes and their structure sheaves. The generic prime of a domain and the nilpotent class in \(k[\epsilon]/(\epsilon^2)\) explain why maximal points alone cannot support all of modern algebraic geometry. Then study morphism properties—finite, proper, smooth, and étale—before drawing global conclusions from a local Jacobian calculation.
