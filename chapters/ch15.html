A cusp has a single visible limiting tangent line, yet its Zariski tangent space is two-dimensional. A node has two visible tangent directions and the same tangent-space dimension. Algebraic tangent spaces measure first-order solutions of the equations, not the number of branches in a drawing. This distinction makes nonsingularity computable and intrinsic.

Lecture spine: L39–42. The chapter follows the Jacobian definition, its local-ring interpretation, the closed singular locus, and the function-field reduction to a hypersurface. It corrects two potentially misleading pictures: a conormal covector is not a canonical perpendicular vector over an arbitrary field, and a singular locus is not a manifold boundary. Default \(k\) is algebraically closed; the cusp–node comparison uses characteristic zero.

15.1 First-order functions without limits L39 40:00

Fix a point \(p\) of an irreducible variety \(X\), and put \(R=\mathcal O_{X,p}\). Its unique maximal ideal \(\mathfrak m\) consists of germs vanishing at \(p\). The quotient \(R/\mathfrak m\cong k\) records values. The quotient \(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2\) records the first-order part of a vanishing germ: products of two vanishing germs are discarded. It is a vector space over \(k\), because multiplying by two germs with the same value changes a vanishing germ only by an element of \(\mathfrak m^2\).

Definition 15.1. The cotangent space at \(p\) is \(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2\). Its dual vector space \[ T_pX=\operatorname{Hom}_k(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2,k) \] is the Zariski tangent space. A tangent vector acts on the first-order part of a function and returns a directional derivative.

This definition does not require a metric or a limit. A tangent vector can equivalently be represented by a derivation at \(p\), a \(k\)-linear map \(D:R\to k\) with \[ D(ab)=a(p)D(b)+b(p)D(a). \] It kills constants and products of two elements of \(\mathfrak m\), so it factors through \(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2\). Conversely a linear functional on that quotient defines \(D(a)\) by applying it to the class of \(a-a(p)\). Expanding \((a-a(p))(b-b(p))\) verifies the product rule, establishing the equivalence.

A third description uses the dual numbers \(k[\epsilon]/(\epsilon^2)\). A map \(a\mapsto a(p)+\epsilon D(a)\) preserves multiplication precisely when \(D\) satisfies the product rule. Its reduction modulo \(\epsilon\) is evaluation at \(p\). Thus a tangent vector is a first-order infinitesimal displacement of the point. The element \(\epsilon\) is nonzero but has square zero; it retains exactly the order that ordinary point-set geometry discarded in Chapter 1.

On \(\mathbb A^1\) at zero, \(\mathfrak m=(t)\), and every germ has a first-order expansion \(a+bt\) modulo \(t^2\). Consequently the cotangent space has basis the class of \(t\), and the tangent space is one-dimensional. On the cusp at its origin, both \(x\) and \(y\) survive independently to first order, as the Jacobian calculation below will show.

15.2 Deriving the Jacobian formula L40 20:00

Let \(X=V(I)\subseteq\mathbb A^n\), with \(I=I(X)\) generated by \(f_1,\ldots,f_r\), and write \(p=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)\). In the ambient polynomial ring \(S=k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\), let \(M=(x_1-a_1,\ldots,x_n-a_n)\). Translating variables and expanding a polynomial into monomials gives

\[ h\equiv h(p)+\sum_{j=1}^n\frac{\partial h}{\partial x_j}(p)(x_j-a_j) \pmod{M^2}. \]

This is a formal polynomial identity, valid in every characteristic. On \(M\), the gradient map is surjective onto \(k^n\), and its kernel is \(M^2\): the linear coefficients all vanish exactly when the polynomial has only terms of degree at least two after translation. The restriction to \(M\) is essential. On the entire polynomial ring, constants also lie in the kernel, so that kernel is not merely \(M^2\).

Passing to the quotient by \(I\) removes the linear relations supplied by the equations. Localizing at the point does not change this first-order quotient. Indeed a denominator \(s\) with \(s(p)\ne0\) acts on \(M/M^2\) as multiplication by the scalar \(s(p)\); its inverse therefore acts by \(1/s(p)\). Thus

\[ \mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2\cong M/(I+M^2). \]

The image of \(I\) in \(M/M^2\) is spanned by the gradients of its generators. For \(h=\sum_i b_if_i\), the product rule and \(f_i(p)=0\) give \(dh(p)=\sum_i b_i(p)\,df_i(p)\). The coefficients reduce to scalars at \(p\); no additional linear relation is created by arbitrary polynomial multiples. Define the Jacobian matrix \(J(p)\) to have entries \(J_{ij}(p)=\partial f_i/\partial x_j(p)\). Rank–nullity now yields

Proposition 15.2. \[ \dim_k T_pX=\dim_k(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2) =n-\operatorname{rank}J(p), \qquad T_pX=\{v\in k^n:J(p)v=0\}. \]

The preceding quotient starts with the \(n\)-dimensional space \(M/M^2\) and divides by the span of the Jacobian rows, of dimension \(\operatorname{rank}J(p)\). Dualizing identifies tangent vectors with the vectors annihilated by those rows. Equivalently substitute \(x_j=a_j+\epsilon v_j\) into each equation and discard \(\epsilon^2\): the surviving condition is \(\sum_j(\partial f_i/\partial x_j)(p)v_j=0\). ∎

The row space consists of the linear forms annihilating the tangent space, a conormal subspace of the ambient cotangent space. At a singular point it must not be confused with the full module of defining equations modulo their products. Identifying it with perpendicular vectors would require additional nondegenerate bilinear or Hermitian geometry. The kernel formula needs none. It also proves independence from the selected generators, because the quotient \(M/(I+M^2)\) depends on the ideal, not its presentation. The local-ring definition proves independence from the affine embedding itself.

An external comparison is Vakil's Stanford-hosted Class 15 notes, pp. 1–3, which relate the same Jacobian rank to the cotangent quotient. The polynomial expansion above gives a direct calculation in the coordinates used in this course.

15.3 Regular local rings and nonsingularity L39 45:00

A Noetherian local ring \(R\), with maximal ideal \(\mathfrak m\) and residue field \(k\), has embedding dimension \(e=\dim_k(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2)\). This is the minimum number of generators of \(\mathfrak m\). One direction is immediate: generators descend to a spanning set. Conversely lifts of a basis generate \(\mathfrak m\) by Nakayama's lemma.

Here is the needed form of that lemma. If a finite \(R\)-module \(N\) satisfies \(N=\mathfrak mN\), choose generators and express each as a linear combination of those generators with coefficients in \(\mathfrak m\). In matrix form \((I-C)v=0\). The determinant of \(I-C\) is 1 modulo \(\mathfrak m\), hence a unit. Multiplying by the adjugate proves every generator zero. Apply this argument to \(\mathfrak m\) modulo the ideal generated by the chosen lifts: its reduction modulo \(\mathfrak m\) is zero, so the quotient itself is zero.

The generalized Krull height theorem gives \(\dim R\le e\), because the maximal ideal is generated by \(e\) elements. This is an invoked commutative-algebra result; see Stacks, Section 10.60 for the dimension and generator bounds. It prevents a tangent space from having fewer dimensions than the local variety.

Definition 15.3. A Noetherian local ring is regular when \(e=\dim R\). A variety \(X\) is nonsingular at \(p\) when \(\mathcal O_{X,p}\) is regular, and singular at \(p\) otherwise. It is nonsingular if every point is nonsingular.

The generator formulation is the definition used in Stacks, Definition 10.60.10. At a closed point of our irreducible \(d\)-dimensional variety, the finite-type dimension theorem gives \(\dim\mathcal O_{X,p}=d\). Combining the definition with Proposition 15.2 proves the classical Jacobian criterion in this setting:

\[ p\text{ nonsingular} \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \dim_k T_pX=d \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \operatorname{rank}J(p)=n-d. \]

The equations must generate the actual vanishing ideal, or an equivalent reduced local presentation. Replacing a defining equation by its square preserves its points but can erase every first derivative there. For example \(V(y)=V(y^2)\) as a set; using \(y^2\) alone at a point of the line produces a zero derivative and a false singularity diagnosis for the reduced line.

A field is perfect if it has characteristic zero, or has positive characteristic \(p\) and every element is a \(p\)th power. In scheme terminology, smoothness over \(k\) is a relative condition on \(X\to\operatorname{Spec}k\). For finite-type varieties over a perfect field, regularity and smoothness agree. Algebraically closed fields are perfect, so the words agree for the varieties in this course. Over an imperfect field they can differ. If \(k\) has characteristic \(p\) and \(a\notin k^p\), the field \(k[u]/(u^p-a)\) is a regular zero-dimensional ring, but the extension is inseparable and is not smooth over \(k\); after adjoining a \(p\)th root it becomes nonreduced.

15.4 The cusp and the node have different branch geometry L39 25:00

For the next computations take characteristic zero. The cusp \(C:y^2-x^3=0\) has Jacobian row \((-3x^2,2y)\). Both entries vanish on \(C\) only at the origin. Elsewhere the rank is one, so the tangent space has dimension \(2-1=1\); at zero it has dimension \(2-0=2\). The curve remains one-dimensional at every point. Its origin local ring is therefore not regular.

The relation \(y^2-x^3\) has no linear term, so it creates no relation between the classes of \(x,y\) in \(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2\). Every pair \((u,v)\in k^2\) gives an infinitesimal solution \(x=\epsilon u,y=\epsilon v\), because both terms in the equation vanish modulo \(\epsilon^2\). This is the full tangent plane, not just the visible limiting tangent line \(y=0\). The latter comes from the lowest-degree homogeneous part \(y^2\) and belongs to the different notion of a tangent cone.

For the node \(N:y^2-x^2(x+1)=0\), the row is \((-3x^2-2x,2y)\). A singular point must have \(y=0\). The curve equation then gives \(x=0\) or \(x=-1\); at \(-1\) the first derivative is \(-1\), so only the origin is singular. Its tangent space again has dimension two, but its lowest-degree equation is \(y^2-x^2=(y-x)(y+x)\), two distinct tangent lines. Tangent dimension alone detects singularity without classifying its type.

The interactive cusp–node figure compares these parametrized curves and their nonsingular parameter values. The accompanying exact algebra laboratory computes Jacobian ranks. A drawing of the real locus helps distinguish branches; the equations and local algebra establish the statements over \(k\).

The lecture's conic \(x^2+y^2=1\) illustrates the characteristic restriction. If the characteristic is not two, its gradient \((2x,2y)\) can vanish only at zero, which is not on the conic, so every point is nonsingular. In characteristic two the displayed polynomial is \((x+y+1)^2\); the actual reduced zero locus is a line. One must replace the squared presentation by its radical before applying the reduced-variety criterion.

Changing the embedding does not change the answer. The affine line embedded as \((t,t^2,t^3)\) has equations \(y-x^2=z-x^3=0\) in three-space. At zero the Jacobian has rows \((0,1,0)\), \((0,0,1)\), of rank two. Its tangent dimension is \(3-2=1\). The ambient dimension and matrix rank both increased, while the intrinsic tangent dimension stayed fixed.

15.5 Singular points form a closed subset L41 25:00

Let \(X\subseteq\mathbb A^n\) be irreducible of dimension \(d\), and put \(c=n-d\). Define \(\operatorname{Sing}X\) to be its singular points. The criterion says these are the points where \(\operatorname{rank}J(p)\lt c\). A matrix has rank below \(c\) exactly when all its \(c\times c\) minor determinants vanish. These determinants are polynomials in the coordinates, so

\[ \operatorname{Sing}X= V\bigl(I(X)+I_c(J)\bigr), \]

where \(I_c(J)\) denotes the ideal generated by those minors. This formula is a statement about a zero locus; one must take its radical to obtain the full vanishing ideal of the singular set. When \(c=0\), the convention that the empty minor is 1 gives the empty singular locus for affine space.

The required size is the codimension \(c\), not necessarily the largest square minors permitted by the displayed matrix. A redundant presentation can have many equations while its generic Jacobian rank is only \(c\). If one checked only larger minors, they would vanish everywhere and incorrectly label the whole variety singular.

For a general variety, apply the affine calculation on a cover. A subset is closed if its intersection with every member of an open cover is relatively closed. Since local rings do not change on passing to an open neighborhood, these affine singular sets agree on overlaps. Thus \(\operatorname{Sing}X\) is closed globally. Its complement \(X_{\mathrm{reg}}\) is open; proving that it is nonempty requires a further argument.

15.6 A dense open hypersurface model L42 10:00

A field is perfect if it has characteristic zero, or has positive characteristic \(p\) and every element is a \(p\)th power. Let \(K=k(X)\) have transcendence degree \(d\) over perfect \(k\). The separating-transcendence-basis theorem gives algebraically independent \(u_1,\ldots,u_d\in K\) such that \(K/k(u_1,\ldots,u_d)\) is finite and separable. This is an invoked field-theory theorem; Conrad's Stanford handout provides a proof, including the positive-characteristic case.

The primitive-element theorem gives \(z\in K\) with \(K=k(u_1,\ldots,u_d)(z)\). Let \(m(T)\) be its irreducible minimal polynomial over that rational-function field. Clear coefficient denominators and remove their common polynomial factor. Gauss's lemma then produces an irreducible polynomial \(F(U_1,\ldots,U_d,Z)\in k[U_1,\ldots,U_d,Z]\). Substitution \(U_i\mapsto u_i,Z\mapsto z\) has kernel \((F)\): after passing to the rational-function coefficient field this is the minimal-polynomial ideal, and primitivity contracts it back to \((F)\).

Thus the hypersurface \(H=V(F)\subseteq\mathbb A^{d+1}\) has function field \(K\), and Chapter 14 makes \(X\) birational to \(H\). Separability says \(m'(z)\ne0\); hence \(\partial F/\partial Z\) is not zero as a function on \(H\). On its nonempty nonvanishing locus, the Jacobian row has rank one and \(H\) is nonsingular. Intersect that locus with the nonempty open participating in the birational isomorphism. Irreducibility makes the intersection nonempty. Its inverse image gives a nonempty open nonsingular subset of \(X\).

Theorem 15.4. Every irreducible variety over an algebraically closed field has a nonempty dense open nonsingular locus. It has a nonempty open subset isomorphic to an open subset of a hypersurface at nonsingular points.

The preceding construction supplies one nonempty open nonsingular subset. Section 15.5 shows that the entire nonsingular locus is open, so it contains that subset and is nonempty. Every nonempty open of an irreducible space is dense. The same birational construction establishes the hypersurface statement. ∎

For hypersurfaces there is also a direct density check. If every point of \(V(F)\) were singular, every partial derivative would vanish on it. The Nullstellensatz and irreducibility imply \(F\) divides each partial derivative. Since every nonzero derivative has smaller degree, all derivatives would be zero polynomials. In characteristic zero this forces \(F\) constant. In characteristic \(p\), it forces every monomial exponent divisible by \(p\); perfectness allows \(p\)th roots of the coefficients, giving \(F=G^p\), contrary to irreducibility. This proves why a reduced irreducible hypersurface cannot be singular everywhere in the stated field setting.

The scheme formulation identifying the regular and smooth loci over a perfect field appears in Stacks, Lemma 33.25.8. Neither that theorem nor the hypersurface construction asserts that the whole variety is globally a hypersurface or that all of its singularities disappear under a global isomorphism.

15.7 What “manifold” means over the complex numbers L41 35:00

When \(k=\mathbb C\), the nonsingular locus is a complex analytic manifold of complex dimension \(d\), hence real dimension \(2d\). This uses the analytic implicit-function theorem and the comparison of the algebraic and analytic local equations. The Zariski tangent calculation identifies the rank condition; the analytic theorem supplies local analytic coordinates. These are different assertions with compatible hypotheses.

The singular set is a closed algebraic complement of a dense open manifold. It is not a boundary in the definition of a manifold with boundary, whose local charts are half-spaces. The complex cusp can even be homeomorphic to a complex line as a topological space while remaining algebraically and analytically singular at its origin. A topological drawing alone cannot certify regularity of its local ring. The correct conclusion is that varieties have dense nonsingular open subsets, with possible singularities in the remaining closed subset.

15.8 Exercises and complete solutions

  1. Compute the tangent spaces of the parabola \(y-x^2=0\) at the origin and at \((1,1)\).
  2. For the cone \(z^2-x^2-y^2=0\) over characteristic zero, find its singular locus and tangent dimension there.
  3. Explain why the polynomial \((x+y+1)^2\) gives a misleading Jacobian test for its reduced zero locus in characteristic two.
  4. The ideal \((y-x^2,z-x^3)\) defines the twisted cubic's affine parametrized curve. Add the redundant generator \(x(y-x^2)\) and verify that the tangent space is unchanged.
  5. Why must the tangent space be the dual of \(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2\), rather than identifying these spaces without a choice?
  6. For the cusp origin, exhibit an infinitesimal tangent direction not belonging to its visible tangent line.
  7. Show directly that an irreducible polynomial over a perfect field cannot have every formal partial derivative zero.
Worked solutions

1. The Jacobian row is \((-2x,1)\). At zero its kernel is \(\{(u,0):u\in k\}\), the horizontal line. At \((1,1)\) its kernel satisfies \(-2u+v=0\), so it is spanned by \((1,2)\). Rank is one at both points; the curve has dimension one and is nonsingular everywhere because the second derivative entry is the constant 1.

2. The gradient is \((-2x,-2y,2z)\), vanishing only at zero. The origin lies on the cone and has a zero Jacobian row, hence tangent space \(k^3\). The hypersurface has dimension two, so this point is singular. Every other point has rank one and tangent dimension two. The smooth complement is a dense open, but its missing vertex is not a manifold boundary chart.

3. In characteristic two the formal derivatives of the square are all zero. However its ordinary zero locus equals the line \(x+y+1=0\), whose vanishing ideal is generated by the unsquared linear polynomial. That generator has gradient \((1,1)\), of rank one. The reduced line is nonsingular. The squared equation describes extra nonreduced structure that the original point set does not record.

4. Write \(f_1=y-x^2\), \(f_2=z-x^3\), \(f_3=xf_1\). At a point of the curve, \(df_3=x\,df_1+f_1\,dx=x\,df_1\). Its Jacobian row therefore lies in the span of the first row at every point. Adding it changes the displayed number of equations but not the row rank or the tangent kernel. This calculation is the generator-independence mechanism in the proof of Proposition 15.2.

5. A class in \(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2\) is the linear part of a function, analogous to a differential. A tangent vector evaluates that linear part. Their natural relationship is evaluation, which pairs a vector space with its dual. They have equal finite dimensions but no canonical identification unless additional data, such as a chosen basis or nondegenerate pairing, is supplied.

6. Take \(x=0,y=\epsilon\) in \(k[\epsilon]/(\epsilon^2)\). Then \(y^2-x^3=0\), so the vertical vector \((0,1)\) is a Zariski tangent vector. It does not satisfy the visible tangent-line equation \(y=0\). The infinitesimal first-order test sees more directions than limiting secants for a singular curve; the tangent cone captures a different constraint.

7. In characteristic zero a monomial containing a variable to positive exponent contributes a nonzero derivative in that variable, and different monomials cannot cancel after differentiation in that variable. Thus all derivatives zero forces a constant. In characteristic \(p\), vanishing of every derivative means every monomial exponent is divisible by \(p\). Perfectness supplies a \(p\)th root for each coefficient. The Frobenius identity \((a+b)^p=a^p+b^p\) then writes the whole polynomial as a \(p\)th power. A nonconstant \(p\)th power is reducible, a contradiction.
