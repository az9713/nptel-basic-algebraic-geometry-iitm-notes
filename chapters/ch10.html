The equation \(y=x^2\) becomes \(YZ=X^2\) after adding a coordinate \(Z\), but the new homogeneous polynomials are not scalar-valued functions on projective space. Their equal-degree ratios are. Graded rings keep track of the scaling, and degree-zero localization removes it on each affine chart.

10.1 Graded rings and homogeneous ideals L24 00:00

Definition 10.1. An \(\mathbb N\)-graded ring is a ring \(S=\bigoplus_{d\ge0}S_d\), where the \(S_d\) are additive subgroups and \(S_aS_b\subseteq S_{a+b}\). An element of \(S_d\) is homogeneous of degree \(d\). An ideal \(J\subseteq S\) is homogeneous if \(J=\bigoplus_{d\ge0}(J\cap S_d)\).

The direct sum means that every element is a finite sum of uniquely determined degree pieces. In the standard example \(S=k[X_0,\ldots,X_n]\), \(S_d\) consists of homogeneous degree-\(d\) polynomials, \(S_0=k\), and multiplication adds total degrees. For a homogeneous ideal, membership of \(f=\sum f_d\) forces membership of every \(f_d\) separately.

Proposition 10.2. An ideal of a graded ring is homogeneous if and only if it can be generated by homogeneous elements. In that case the quotient inherits the grading \((S/J)_d=S_d/(J\cap S_d)\).

If \(J\) is homogeneous, take all its homogeneous elements as generators; every element is a finite sum of them. Conversely, suppose the generators \(h_\alpha\) are homogeneous. In a finite expression \(\sum a_\alpha h_\alpha\), decompose each coefficient \(a_\alpha\) into homogeneous pieces. Collecting terms of a fixed degree expresses that degree component as a sum of elements of \(J\). Thus all degree pieces remain in \(J\). The quotient decomposition is direct because an equality between different degree pieces modulo \(J\) splits degree by degree. Multiplication continues to add degrees. ∎

The ideal \((X_0+X_1^2)\) is not homogeneous: it does not contain its generator's linear component \(X_0\). The ideal \((X_0,X_1^2)\) is homogeneous. Homogeneity concerns the whole ideal, not whether one chosen generating list happens to look tidy.

Sums, products and intersections of homogeneous ideals are homogeneous. Their radicals are homogeneous too. For the latter, if \(f=\sum_{d=0}^D f_d\) has a power in a homogeneous ideal \(J\), the highest-degree piece of that power is \(f_D^m\), so \(f_D\in\sqrt J\). Subtract \(f_D\) from \(f\) in the ideal \(\sqrt J\), and repeat on the remaining degrees. This proves that every component of every element of \(\sqrt J\) is again in \(\sqrt J\).

10.2 The irrelevant ideal and the projective dictionary L24 30:00

Let \(S=k[X_0,\ldots,X_n]\) with its standard grading. Its irrelevant ideal is \(S_+=(X_0,\ldots,X_n)=\bigoplus_{d\gt 0}S_d\). In affine \((n+1)\)-space it cuts out the origin; in projective space its zero locus is empty, because a projective tuple cannot have all coordinates zero.

For \(T\subseteq\mathbb P^n\), let \(I_+(T)\) be the ideal generated by all homogeneous polynomials vanishing at every point of \(T\). It is homogeneous. If \(T\ne\varnothing\), its elements have no nonzero constant component. For a homogeneous ideal \(J\), its projective zero set is denoted \(V_+(J)\). Its affine cone is \(V(J)\subseteq\mathbb A^{n+1}\): for a proper \(J\), this is the origin together with all lines above \(V_+(J)\).

Theorem 10.3 — homogeneous Nullstellensatz. If \(F\) is homogeneous of positive degree, then \[ F|_{V_+(J)}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F\in\sqrt J. \] If \(V_+(J)\ne\varnothing\), then \(I_+(V_+(J))=\sqrt J\). If \(V_+(J)=\varnothing\), its vanishing ideal is \(S\), and \(\sqrt J\) is either \(S_+\) or \(S\).

A positive-degree homogeneous polynomial vanishes at the origin. Therefore vanishing on the projective zero locus is equivalent to vanishing on its entire affine cone. The affine Nullstellensatz from Chapter 1 gives \(F\in\sqrt J\). The converse follows by evaluating a power at each point. If the projective zero locus is nonempty, no nonzero constant vanishes on it, and the equality extends degree by degree to the whole homogeneous ideal. If it is empty, the affine cone is either only the origin or empty; the affine Nullstellensatz gives radical \(S_+\) or \(S\), respectively. ∎

Thus nonempty projective algebraic sets correspond to proper radical homogeneous ideals different from \(S_+\). Include the empty set by assigning it the unit ideal \(S\), not \(S_+\). Both ideals have empty projective zero locus, so keeping both would destroy injectivity of the dictionary.

A nonempty projective algebraic set is irreducible exactly when its homogeneous vanishing ideal is prime. One can repeat Chapter 2's product argument with homogeneous equations, or use its irreducible cone. A projective point does not correspond to a maximal ideal in \(S\). For example, \([1:a]\in\mathbb P^1\) has ideal \((X_1-aX_0)\), with quotient \(k[X_0]\); its cone is a line. The maximal homogeneous ideal \(S_+\) instead corresponds to the excluded affine origin.

For general graded rings the homogeneous primes not containing the irrelevant ideal form the space called \(\operatorname{Proj}S\). Its construction and the degree-zero localizations below are developed in Stacks, Section 27.8, “Proj of a graded ring”. Here we use the classical projective points and the affine charts explicitly, rather than assume scheme theory.

10.3 Projective and quasi-projective functions L25 15:00

A projective variety is a nonempty irreducible closed subset of some \(\mathbb P^n\). A quasi-projective variety is a nonempty open subset of a projective variety, equivalently an irreducible locally closed subset of projective space. Here locally closed means the intersection of an open and a closed subset. Affine and quasi-affine varieties are included by placing \(\mathbb A^n\) in the projective chart \(X_0\ne0\) and taking projective closure.

A scalar-valued function is regular at a point of such a variety when, on a neighborhood, it is a quotient \(F/G\) of homogeneous polynomials of the same degree, with \(G\) nonvanishing on that neighborhood. Equal degrees cancel the scaling factor. Sums and products retain the form after bringing denominators to common degree, and reciprocal functions are regular where their numerators do not vanish.

This agrees with regularity in affine chart coordinates. On \(U_i\), a quotient \(F/G\) becomes \(F(\ldots,1,\ldots)/G(\ldots,1,\ldots)\). Conversely, a fraction \(a/b\) in affine coordinates can be homogenized by multiplying the numerator or denominator by additional powers of \(X_i\) until their degrees agree. On \(U_i\), those powers do not create zeros in a denominator. Thus the projective definition glues the previously established affine one.

Morphisms still mean continuous maps pulling regular functions on open subsets back to regular functions. Theorem 8.3 continues to hold for any quasi-projective source and affine target; its proof uses the source's regular functions, not affineness of that source. Maps into a projective target can instead be checked locally in its affine charts.

Every variety in this course has a finite affine open cover. A closed projective variety intersects each \(U_i\) in an affine algebraic set, irreducible when nonempty. An open subset of such a piece is a union of principal opens, each affine by Chapter 6. Noetherian quasi-compactness reduces this to finitely many. This is the precise sense in which affine varieties are the local building blocks even when the global variety is not affine.

The ring \(S(X)=S/I_+(X)\) is the homogeneous coordinate ring of a specified projective embedding. Its degree-one elements do not define scalar-valued global functions on \(X\); they transform under rescaling. In particular \(S(X)\) is not \(\mathcal O_X(X)\). Chapter 13 proves that the latter is \(k\) for irreducible projective \(X\), and explains how a change of embedding can change \(S(X)\).

10.4 Homogenization and closure L26 25:00

Choose the affine chart \(X_0\ne0\), with coordinates \(t_j=X_j/X_0\). For a nonzero polynomial \(g(t_1,\ldots,t_n)\) of total degree \(d\), its homogenization is \[ g^{\mathrm h}(X_0,\ldots,X_n)=X_0^d\,g(X_1/X_0,\ldots,X_n/X_0). \] Each term has degree \(d\), and all denominators cancel. Dehomogenization of a homogeneous \(F\) sets \(X_0=1\): \(F^{\mathrm{dh}}(t)=F(1,t)\). The zero polynomial homogenizes to zero by convention.

Dehomogenizing \(g^{\mathrm h}\) recovers \(g\). In the reverse direction a power of \(X_0\) can disappear: \(X_0X_1\) dehomogenizes to \(t_1\), whose homogenization is only \(X_1\). This is appropriate on the chart where \(X_0\) is invertible. Bare homogenization is not a ring homomorphism: \(t+(-t+1)=1\), whereas the two degree-one homogenizations sum to \(X_0\), not to 1. The fractions in the next section repair this mismatch.

For the vanishing ideal \(I=I(Z)\) of an affine algebraic set \(Z\), homogenize every element and take their generated homogeneous ideal \(I^{\mathrm h}\). Its zero locus is the projective closure of \(Z\). Indeed, every homogeneous equation vanishing on \(Z\) in the chart dehomogenizes to an element \(g\in I\). If the original equation has degree \(d\) and \(g\) has degree \(e\), the equation is \(X_0^{d-e}g^{\mathrm h}\), so it belongs to \(I^{\mathrm h}\). Thus \(I^{\mathrm h}\) supplies exactly all homogeneous equations vanishing on \(Z\), and their common zero locus is its smallest projective closed container. The same reasoning shows \(X_0\)-saturation: if \(X_0^mH\in I^{\mathrm h}\) with \(H\) homogeneous, then \(H^{\mathrm{dh}}\in I\), and rehomogenization puts \(H\) itself in \(I^{\mathrm h}\).

Why selected generators can mislead. In \(\mathbb A^3\), the equations \(xy=1\) and \(xz=1\) imply \(y=z\), since \[ z(xy-1)-y(xz-1)=y-z. \] In projective coordinates \([W:X:Y:Z]\), homogenizing only the two displayed generators gives \(XY-W^2=0\), \(XZ-W^2=0\). At \(W=X=0\), every \([0:0:Y:Z]\) satisfies them, an entire extra line. The homogenization of the additional ideal element \(y-z\) gives \(Y-Z=0\). The actual closure is the conic cut out by \(Y-Z=0\) and \(XY-W^2=0\); its intersection with \(W\ne0\) is the original curve, and at infinity it has only \([0:1:0:0]\) and \([0:0:1:1]\).

Algebraically, saturation with respect to \(X_0\) replaces an ideal \(J\) by \(\{F:X_0^mF\in J\text{ for some }m\ge0\}\). Homogenizing a generating list and then saturating with respect to the chart coordinate gives the ideal obtained by homogenizing the entire affine ideal. It removes components supported only at the excluded chart boundary. Projective closure is more than mechanically adding a variable to each input equation.

10.5 Degree-zero localization recovers the chart L26 45:00 L27 10:00

For a homogeneous element \(f\) of positive degree \(e\) in a graded domain \(S\), the localization \(S_f\) has a \(\mathbb Z\)-grading: a homogeneous fraction \(a/f^m\) has degree \(\deg a-me\). Its degree-zero subring, denoted \(S_{(f)}=(S_f)_0\), consists of sums of fractions whose numerator and denominator degrees agree. These are precisely the expressions invariant under scalar rescaling.

Proposition 10.4. For \(S=k[X_0,\ldots,X_n]\), \[ (S_{X_i})_0=k[X_0/X_i,\ldots,\widehat{X_i/X_i},\ldots,X_n/X_i] \cong k[t_1,\ldots,t_n]. \] This is the ring of regular functions on \(U_i\).

A degree-zero fraction has the form \(F/X_i^d\), with \(F\) homogeneous of degree \(d\). Each monomial becomes a product of the ratios \(X_j/X_i\); hence these ratios generate the ring. They are algebraically independent: substituting \(X_i=1\) into a proposed polynomial relation would give that same relation among \(n\) independent variables. Thus substitution is an isomorphism with a polynomial ring. Chapter 9 identified \(U_i\) with affine \(n\)-space, and Chapter 7 computed all regular functions on an affine, so this ring is \(\mathcal O(U_i)\). ∎

The isomorphism can be written \(g\mapsto g^{\mathrm h}/X_i^{\deg g}\), where the homogenizing coordinate is \(X_i\). Unlike bare homogenization, this is a ring homomorphism because it is exactly substitution \(t_j\mapsto X_j/X_i\). Its inverse sets \(X_i=1\). If a common \(X_i\) factor cancels, the represented degree-zero fraction remains unchanged.

For a projective variety \(X\) with homogeneous coordinate ring \(R=S/I_+(X)\), the same argument gives \[ \mathcal O_X(X\cap U_i)=(R_{X_i})_0 \] whenever the intersection is nonempty. Quotienting imposes the dehomogenized equations; localization and taking degree zero describe functions on that affine chart. The standard generalization is that \(D_+(f)=X\setminus V_+(f)\) is affine with ring \(R_{(f)}\), as in the Proj construction. This general assertion is invoked; the coordinate-chart case has been proved directly.

On \(\mathbb P^1\), take \(f=X_0X_1\). Then \(D_+(f)\) is the overlap of the two charts, with coordinate \(t=X_1/X_0\ne0\). The degree-zero fractions \(X_1^2/f=t\) and \(X_0^2/f=t^{-1}\) generate its ring \(k[t,t^{-1}]\). The full localization \(S_f\) still remembers scale; its degree-zero part remembers only the projective point.

This explains the title of L27: starting from a polynomial ring in \(n\) variables, adding a homogenizing variable and then homogeneously localizing at it recovers a polynomial ring in \(n\) affine ratios. The new variable is not discarded from the projective completion. It remains essential for the boundary \(X_i=0\), which that one chart cannot see.

10.6 Exercises and complete solutions

  1. Compute \(V_+((X_0^2,X_1^3))\subseteq\mathbb P^1\), its vanishing ideal, and the radical of the original ideal.
  2. Find the affine equation of \(X_0X_2-X_1^2=0\) in each of the charts \(X_0\ne0\) and \(X_2\ne0\).
  3. Prove that a proper homogeneous ideal is prime if the prime-product condition holds for homogeneous factors.
  4. Homogenize \(g(t)=t^2+t+1\), then write the associated degree-zero fraction and recover \(g\) by dehomogenization.
  5. Explain why \(X_0/X_1\) belongs to \((k[X_0,X_1]_{X_1})_0\), while \(1/X_1\) does not.
Worked solutions

1. Both coordinates would have to vanish, so the projective zero locus is empty. Its vanishing ideal is the whole ring \(S=k[X_0,X_1]\). The radical of the original ideal is instead \(S_+=(X_0,X_1)\): each variable has a power in the ideal, and the ideal is contained in this radical maximal ideal. This is the exceptional case in the projective dictionary.

2. In \(X_0\ne0\), set \(u=X_1/X_0\), \(v=X_2/X_0\). Division by \(X_0^2\) gives \(v=u^2\). In \(X_2\ne0\), set \(a=X_0/X_2\), \(b=X_1/X_2\); division by \(X_2^2\) gives \(a=b^2\). Each piece is an affine parabola, hence an affine line. On the overlap \(v\ne0\), the coordinates relate by \(a=1/v\), \(b=u/v\).

3. The quotient \(S/J\) is graded. The hypothesis says that the product of two nonzero homogeneous elements of this quotient is nonzero. If two arbitrary nonzero elements had zero product, choose their lowest-degree nonzero components, of degrees \(r,s\). The degree-\((r+s)\) component of their product is exactly the product of those two components, because no lower-degree nonzero components exist. It is nonzero by hypothesis, a contradiction. Thus the quotient is a domain and \(J\) is prime, assuming \(J\) is proper.

4. With homogenizing variable \(X_0\), \(g^{\mathrm h}=X_1^2+X_0X_1+X_0^2\). The degree-zero fraction is \(g^{\mathrm h}/X_0^2=(X_1/X_0)^2+X_1/X_0+1\). Setting \(X_0=1\), \(X_1=t\) recovers \(t^2+t+1\). The quotient is the regular function on the chart, whereas \(g^{\mathrm h}\) alone has a scale-dependent value.

5. The first fraction has numerator and denominator degrees both one, so its degree is zero and its value is scale-invariant. The second has degree minus one and transforms as \(\lambda^{-1}/X_1\) under rescaling by \(\lambda\). It is a regular function on the punctured affine cone where \(X_1\ne0\), but not a scalar-valued function on the corresponding projective chart.

Boundary: homogeneous coordinate rings refer to specified embeddings. General Proj language is a reference extension, not an assumed prerequisite. Projective emptiness, saturation at a chart boundary, and equal-degree fractions are separate issues; none can be dropped when translating between equations and projective geometry.
