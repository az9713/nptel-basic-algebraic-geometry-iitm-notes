The crossing \(xy=0\) is connected: its two axes meet. Yet it consists of two algebraic pieces. To identify the objects that function as single geometric components, connectedness must be strengthened to irreducibility. The matching algebraic condition is primality.

Lecture spine: L4–5, including point ideals, the radical dictionary, irreducibility and its topological properties, and the equivalence with prime ideals. Examples and proofs are independent exposition. All spaces called irreducible are required to be nonempty.

2.1 Points, maximal ideals, and intermediate objects L4 05:00

For a point \(a\in\mathbb A^n\), evaluation \(f\mapsto f(a)\) is a surjective \(k\)-algebra homomorphism from \(k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\) to \(k\). A \(k\)-algebra homomorphism is a ring homomorphism that fixes the embedded copy of \(k\). Its kernel is \(\mathfrak m_a=(x_1-a_1,\ldots,x_n-a_n)\). One can verify the kernel description by successively subtracting the value in each coordinate; each difference is divisible by \(x_i-a_i\). The quotient is a field, so the ideal is maximal.

Algebraic closure supplies the converse, as established in Chapter 1: each maximal ideal has this form. If \(I\subseteq\mathfrak m_a\), then \(a\in V(I)\). Thus incidence between a point and an algebraic set is ideal containment in the opposite direction. A line through the origin in the plane corresponds to an ideal strictly between \((0)\) and \((x,y)\). Which of these intermediate ideals describe indivisible geometric pieces?

An ideal \(P\subsetneq R\) is prime if \(fg\in P\) implies \(f\in P\) or \(g\in P\). Equivalently, \(R/P\) is an integral domain, a nonzero commutative ring without zero divisors. Every maximal ideal is prime, but a prime ideal need not be maximal. The ideal \((y)\subset k[x,y]\) is prime because its quotient is \(k[x]\); it describes an entire line rather than one point.

2.2 Irreducibility as an intersection property L4 20:00

Definition 2.1. A nonempty topological space \(X\) is irreducible if \(X=F_1\cup F_2\), with \(F_i\) closed in \(X\), implies \(X=F_1\) or \(X=F_2\). A subset is irreducible if it is irreducible with its induced topology. A closed irreducible subset of affine space is an affine variety in our convention.

The induced topology matters. Closed subsets of \(Y\subseteq X\) have the form \(Y\cap F\) with \(F\) closed in \(X\). When \(Y\) is not closed in \(X\), a relatively closed subset need not be closed in the ambient space. Statements about reducibility always concern the topology on the object itself.

Proposition 2.2. A nonempty space \(X\) is irreducible if and only if any two nonempty open subsets intersect. In that case every nonempty open subset is dense and irreducible.

Disjoint nonempty open sets \(U,V\) give \(X=(X\setminus U)\cup(X\setminus V)\), a union of two proper closed subsets. Conversely a union of two proper closed subsets gives disjoint nonempty complements. This proves the equivalence. If a nonempty open \(U\) were not dense, \(X\setminus\overline U\) would be a nonempty open disjoint from \(U\), impossible. For irreducibility of \(U\), two nonempty relatively open subsets of \(U\) are also open in \(X\), since \(U\) is open. They must intersect. ∎

This is much stronger than connectedness. A connected space has no decomposition into two disjoint nonempty open subsets covering it. An irreducible space forbids even two disjoint nonempty opens that fail to cover the space. A Euclidean interval is connected but reducible, since two overlapping proper closed subintervals can cover it. On an infinite affine line with the Zariski topology, every nonempty open is cofinite, so any two nonempty opens intersect.

Irreducibility also survives closure and continuous images. If \(Y\) is irreducible and \(\overline Y=F_1\cup F_2\) with relatively closed \(F_i\), intersect with \(Y\); one \(F_i\) contains \(Y\), hence contains its closure. For a continuous map \(f\), a closed decomposition of \(f(Y)\) pulls back to a closed decomposition of \(Y\). Thus the image, and its closure, are irreducible. The image need not be closed.

2.3 The prime–irreducible dictionary L5 20:00

Theorem 2.3. Let \(X\subseteq\mathbb A^n\) be a nonempty algebraic set. Then \(X\) is irreducible if and only if its vanishing ideal \(I(X)\) is prime. Consequently, for an arbitrary proper ideal \(J\), \(V(J)\) is irreducible if and only if \(\sqrt J\) is prime.

Suppose \(X\) is irreducible and \(fg\in I(X)\). Each point of \(X\) is a zero of \(f\) or of \(g\), because evaluation lands in a field. Thus \[ X=(X\cap V(f))\cup(X\cap V(g)). \] Both terms are closed in \(X\). Irreducibility implies one is all of \(X\), so \(f\in I(X)\) or \(g\in I(X)\). The ideal is proper because \(X\ne\varnothing\).

Conversely, suppose \(I(X)\) is prime but \(X=F\cup G\) with \(F,G\) proper relatively closed subsets. They are also closed in affine space, since \(X\) is closed. Choose a point of \(X\setminus F\). Since \(F=V(I(F))\), some \(f\in I(F)\) does not vanish at that point; hence \(f\notin I(X)\). Similarly choose \(g\in I(G)\setminus I(X)\). Their product vanishes on \(F\cup G=X\), contradicting primality. Finally \(I(V(J))=\sqrt J\) by the Nullstellensatz. ∎

The last statement requires the radical. For \(J=(y^2)\), its zero set is the irreducible line \(y=0\), but \(J\) is not prime: \(y\cdot y\in J\) while \(y\notin J\). The prime ideal that geometry detects is \((y)=\sqrt J\).

The theorem can also be read as a statement about functions. On an irreducible algebraic set, the product of two polynomial functions cannot vanish everywhere unless one factor vanishes everywhere. On the crossing \(V(xy)\), the coordinate functions \(x\) and \(y\) violate this property: neither is identically zero, while their product is. Zero divisors in the coordinate ring will record the decomposition.

2.4 The parabola, crossing, and a misleading polynomial

The parabola \(X=V(y-x^2)\) is irreducible. The substitution homomorphism \(k[x,y]\to k[t]\), \(x\mapsto t,y\mapsto t^2\), is surjective and has kernel \((y-x^2)\), by polynomial division in \(y\). Its quotient is the domain \(k[t]\), so the defining ideal is prime. This proof identifies the abstract parabola with an affine line at the level of functions; the morphism-theoretic statement will follow in Chapter 8.

In contrast, the crossing \(V(xy)\) is \(V(x)\cup V(y)\). Both axes are proper closed subsets and meet at the origin. Thus the crossing is reducible even though it is connected. Connectedness follows because each axis is irreducible, hence connected, and a union of connected spaces with a common point is connected: a separation of the union would separate an axis or put the shared point in two disjoint sides.

Over \(\mathbb R\), the polynomial \(x^2+y^2\) is irreducible, but its real zero set is a single point with vanishing ideal \((x,y)\). Over \(\mathbb C\) the polynomial factors as \((x+iy)(x-iy)\), so its complex zero locus is two lines. Neither the field nor the distinction between a polynomial and its vanishing ideal can be suppressed.

Even over \(\mathbb C\), the number of displayed equations does not determine irreducibility. A set given by many equations may be irreducible, and one equation can define a union of many components. The prime ideal, rather than the input's syntax, decides.

2.5 Exercises and complete solutions

  1. Prove that an irreducible Hausdorff space has at most one point. A Hausdorff space is one in which distinct points have disjoint open neighborhoods.
  2. Find the irreducible components of \(V(xy(x-y))\subseteq\mathbb A^2\).
  3. Prove that a finite union of proper closed subsets cannot cover an irreducible space.
  4. Show that two polynomial functions agreeing on a nonempty open subset of an affine variety agree everywhere.
Worked solutions

1. Two distinct points in a Hausdorff space have disjoint nonempty open neighborhoods. Proposition 2.2 forbids this in an irreducible space. This explains why the Zariski topology on a positive-dimensional variety cannot be Hausdorff. It is nevertheless \(T_1\) on classical points, meaning each singleton is closed.

2. The product vanishes precisely on the three lines \(x=0\), \(y=0\), and \(x-y=0\). Each is irreducible, since its coordinate ring is a polynomial ring in one variable. No line is contained in another. An irreducible subset contained in their finite union must lie in one line by repeated use of irreducibility, so these are the maximal irreducible closed subsets.

3. Induct on the number \(r\) of subsets. The case \(r=2\) is the definition. For \(r\gt2\), group the first \(r-1\) closed subsets into one closed subset. If that union is all of \(X\), apply induction; if the last subset is all of \(X\), it was not proper. Either conclusion contradicts the proposed cover by proper subsets.

4. The difference \(f-g\) has a closed zero locus containing a nonempty open set. That open set is dense by Proposition 2.2. A closed set containing a dense subset is the entire variety. Hence \(f-g=0\) as a polynomial function, even though chosen polynomial representatives may differ by an element of \(I(X)\).

Boundary: irreducibility is a topological condition; primality is its algebraic translation for the correct vanishing ideal. Connectedness, irreducibility of a polynomial over a smaller field, and reducedness are distinct conditions. The course's convention reserves “variety” for irreducible objects.
