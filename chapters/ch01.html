Two systems of polynomial equations can describe exactly the same points while generating different ideals. A computer that manipulates equations must therefore know which algebraic information survives when we retain only their common zeros. The first problem is to build a precise dictionary between equations and geometric subsets.

Lecture spine: L1–3. The proofs, computational examples, and exercises below are independently written. Default: \(k\) is an algebraically closed field. The case \(k=\mathbb C\) is a useful model, but the topology here is algebraic rather than Euclidean. Caption evidence establishes the topic sequence; it does not constitute a complete visual review of the blackboard.

1.1 Equations and the choice of field L1 00:00

Consider the parabola \(y=x^2\). The equations \(y-x^2=0\) and \((y-x^2)^2=0\) have the same solutions in any field. Their ideals in \(k[x,y]\) differ: the polynomial \(y-x^2\) belongs to the first ideal and does not belong to the second. A set of points cannot distinguish the multiplicity with which its defining equation was written. This loss of information is the reason radical ideals will replace arbitrary ideals.

A commutative ring with identity has addition, multiplication, additive inverses, and an element 1, with the usual distributive and commutative laws. All ring maps in these notes preserve 1. A field is such a ring in which \(1\ne0\) and every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse. The polynomial ring \(k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\) consists of finite sums of monomials \(a x_1^{i_1}\cdots x_n^{i_n}\), where \(a\in k\) and each exponent is a nonnegative integer.

Definition 1.1. For a set \(S\subseteq k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\), its zero locus is \[ V(S)=\{a=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)\in k^n: f(a)=0\text{ for every }f\in S\}. \] An algebraic set is a subset of this form. We also write \(V(f_1,\ldots,f_r)\) for the zero locus of that finite list.

The field is part of the problem. Over the real numbers, \(V(x^2+1)\) is empty; over the complex numbers it has two points. A field is algebraically closed if every nonconstant polynomial in one variable has a root in it. Repeated division by linear factors then splits every such polynomial completely. Algebraic closure does not make every system consistent: \(V(x,x-1)=\varnothing\) over every field. What prevents consistency is an algebraic certificate that the equations generate 1.

The notation \(\mathbb A_k^n\), or \(\mathbb A^n\) when the field is fixed, means the set \(k^n\) equipped with the Zariski topology defined next. Its points can be added after choosing coordinates, but affine geometry does not require a privileged origin. Translating a parabola changes its equations while preserving its abstract geometric structure.

1.2 Why zero loci define a topology L2 05:00

A topology can be specified by closed subsets: the empty set and whole space are closed, arbitrary intersections of closed sets are closed, and finite unions of closed sets are closed. Declaring the algebraic subsets of \(k^n\) closed gives the Zariski topology. Here are the identities that justify the declaration.

Proposition 1.2. If \(S_\alpha\) are sets of polynomials, then \[ V(0)=k^n,\qquad V(1)=\varnothing,\qquad \bigcap_\alpha V(S_\alpha)=V\!\left(\bigcup_\alpha S_\alpha\right). \] For two ideals \(I,J\subseteq k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\), \[ V(I)\cup V(J)=V(IJ), \] where \(IJ\) is the ideal generated by products \(fg\) with \(f\in I,g\in J\).

The first two identities follow by evaluating the constant polynomials. Membership in the intersection means satisfying every equation in every \(S_\alpha\), exactly the condition on the right. If a point lies in \(V(I)\) or \(V(J)\), each product vanishes there, so it belongs to \(V(IJ)\). Conversely, if a point \(a\) is in neither zero locus, choose \(f\in I\) and \(g\in J\) with \(f(a)\ne0\) and \(g(a)\ne0\). Since \(k\) is a field, their product is nonzero, contradicting \(a\in V(IJ)\). Repeating the argument gives finite unions. ∎

The absence of zero divisors in a field matters in the last step. The construction using ordinary points over a general ring needs more care; scheme theory will instead use prime ideals. The course stays with varieties over an algebraically closed field.

For example, \(V(xy)\subseteq\mathbb A^2\) is the union of the two coordinate axes. An infinite union need not be closed: over \(\mathbb C\), the subset of integer points in \(\mathbb A^1\) is an infinite union of closed singletons but is not closed. A nonzero one-variable polynomial has only finitely many roots, so the only closed set containing all those integers is the entire line. The Zariski closure can be much larger than the Euclidean closure.

A map of topological spaces is continuous if the inverse image of every closed set is closed. A polynomial \(f:k^n\to k\) is Zariski continuous: the inverse image of the zero locus of polynomials \(g_\alpha(t)\) is the common zero locus of the substitutions \(g_\alpha(f(x))\). The same argument works for a tuple of polynomials. Continuity is necessary for an algebraic morphism, but by itself it will be far too weak.

1.3 Finite equations inside infinite ideals L2 30:00

An ideal \(I\) of a ring \(R\) is an additive subgroup closed under multiplication by every element of \(R\). The ideal generated by a subset \(S\), denoted \((S)\), consists of finite sums \(\sum_i g_i f_i\), with \(f_i\in S\) and \(g_i\in R\). The number of terms can vary; every individual sum remains finite. Evaluation shows that \(V(S)=V((S))\): vanishing on the generators implies vanishing on every such combination.

A ring is Noetherian if each ideal is finitely generated. Equivalently, every ascending chain of ideals stabilizes. To prove this equivalence, take the union of an ascending chain; it is an ideal. Its finite generating set lies in a single member of the chain, so all later members coincide. Conversely, if an ideal could not be generated by finitely many elements, successively adjoining an element outside the previous finitely generated ideal would produce a strictly ascending chain.

An \(R\)-module is an abelian additive group with multiplication by scalars in \(R\), satisfying the distributive laws, associativity of scalar multiplication, and \(1m=m\). A submodule is closed under addition, additive inverses, and those scalar products. A module is finitely generated if a finite list generates it by \(R\)-linear combinations. A free module with basis \(e_1,\ldots,e_d\) consists of unique sums \(\sum_i r_ie_i\). Thus polynomials of degree below \(d\) form a finite free \(R\)-module.

Theorem 1.3 — Hilbert basis theorem. If \(R\) is Noetherian, then \(R[t]\) is Noetherian. Consequently, every ideal of \(k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\) has finitely many generators.

For an ideal \(J\subseteq R[t]\), let \(L\) be the ideal of leading coefficients of its nonzero elements, together with zero. It is an ideal because degrees can be aligned by multiplication by powers of \(t\) before addition. Choose finitely many leading coefficients generating \(L\), represented by polynomials \(f_1,\ldots,f_r\in J\). Let \(d\) be the maximum of their degrees. Every element of \(J\) of degree at least \(d\) has its leading term canceled by an \(R[t]\)-linear combination of the \(f_i\). Repeating lowers its degree below \(d\).

The polynomials in \(J\) of degree below \(d\) form an \(R\)-submodule of the finite free module with basis \(1,t,\ldots,t^{d-1}\). Every submodule of a finite free module over a Noetherian ring is finitely generated. To verify this assertion, induct on the number of basis vectors. Project a submodule of \(R^d\) onto its last coordinate; its image is a finitely generated ideal of \(R\). Choose lifts of those generators. The projection kernel is a submodule of \(R^{d-1}\), finitely generated by induction, and its generators together with the chosen lifts generate the whole submodule. The rank-one case is precisely the ideal property of \(R\).

Choose module generators for this low-degree part of \(J\). Together with the \(f_i\), they generate \(J\) as an ideal. The zero ideal is immediate, and if \(d=0\) there is no low-degree part. Induction on the number of variables finishes the theorem because a field has only the ideals \((0)\) and \((1)\). ∎

Thus an algebraic set always has a finite list of defining equations even when originally supplied by infinitely many polynomials. This is an existence statement, not a promise that the input is algorithmically accessible or that a short list is cheap to find. Gröbner-basis computations make finite polynomial ideals executable once generators and effective field arithmetic are available.

1.4 The vanishing ideal and radical closure L3 20:00

For any subset \(T\subseteq k^n\), define its vanishing ideal by \(I(T)=\{f:f(a)=0\text{ for all }a\in T\}\). Addition, additive inverses and multiplication by arbitrary polynomials preserve vanishing, so this is an ideal. More equations produce fewer points; more points impose more vanishing conditions. Formally, \(S\subseteq S'\) implies \(V(S')\subseteq V(S)\), and \(T\subseteq T'\) implies \(I(T')\subseteq I(T)\).

Proposition 1.4. The set \(V(I(T))\) is the Zariski closure \(\overline T\), meaning the smallest closed subset containing \(T\).

Each point of \(T\) annihilates every polynomial in \(I(T)\), so \(T\subseteq V(I(T))\). If \(T\subseteq V(J)\), then \(J\subseteq I(T)\), and order reversal gives \(V(I(T))\subseteq V(J)\). Thus \(V(I(T))\) is contained in every closed set containing \(T\). ∎

The radical of an ideal \(J\) is \(\sqrt J=\{f:f^m\in J\text{ for some integer }m\ge1\}\). It is an ideal: closure under multiplication is immediate; if \(f^r,g^s\in J\), each term of the binomial expansion of \((f+g)^{r+s}\) contains either \(f^r\) or \(g^s\). A radical ideal equals its own radical. Every vanishing ideal is radical, since a field has no nonzero nilpotent elements.

Theorem 1.5 — Nullstellensatz. Over an algebraically closed field, a proper ideal \(J\subsetneq k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\) has a common zero (weak form), and \(I(V(J))=\sqrt J\) (strong form).

The weak form is invoked here as a standard commutative-algebra theorem; a full proof is available in Stacks, Theorem 10.34.1. We derive the strong form from it. The inclusion \(\sqrt J\subseteq I(V(J))\) follows by evaluating a power at each point. For the reverse inclusion, let \(f\) vanish on \(V(J)\). If \(f=0\) there is nothing to prove. Otherwise introduce a new variable \(t\). The equations in \(J\), together with \(1-tf=0\), have no common zero: any solution of \(J\) has \(f=0\). The weak form says that their ideal in \(k[x_1,\ldots,x_n,t]\) is the unit ideal. Therefore \[ 1=\sum_{i=1}^r a_i(x,t)g_i(x)+b(x,t)(1-tf(x)),\qquad g_i\in J. \] Substitute \(t=1/f\) in the ring allowing powers of \(f\) as denominators. The last term vanishes. Clearing the finitely many denominators gives \(f^N\in J\) for some nonnegative integer \(N\); if \(N=0\), then \(J\) is the unit ideal and \(f\in J\) anyway. This proves \(f\in\sqrt J\). ∎

Closed subsets of affine space and radical ideals therefore correspond bijectively, with inclusions reversed. On the parabola, \(I(V((y-x^2)^2))=(y-x^2)\). Over \(\mathbb R\), the same dictionary fails for ordinary real points: \(V(x^2+1)=\varnothing\), whose vanishing ideal is the whole ring, whereas \((x^2+1)\) is a proper radical ideal.

A proper ideal is maximal if no proper ideal strictly contains it. Equivalently its quotient is a field: the absence of intermediate ideals means that every nonzero residue class generates the unit ideal and therefore has an inverse. The weak theorem identifies points with these maximal ideals. For \(a\in k^n\), evaluation at \(a\) has kernel \(\mathfrak m_a=(x_1-a_1,\ldots,x_n-a_n)\) and quotient \(k\), hence a maximal ideal. Conversely, any maximal ideal has a common zero \(a\), is contained in \(\mathfrak m_a\), and equals it by maximality. Geometry has recovered its points from the ring.

1.5 A consistency certificate and a lost multiplicity

In \(k[x,y]\), take \(J=(x,xy-1)\). Since \(y\cdot x-(xy-1)=1\), the equations have no simultaneous solution. This finite identity is stronger computational evidence than searching a grid and failing to find a point. It proves emptiness over every field extension of \(k\).

Now take \(J=(x^2,y)\). Its zero locus is the origin, and \(I(V(J))=(x,y)\). The element \(x\) is not in \(J\), but \(x^2\in J\). In the quotient \(k[x,y]/J\), the class of \(x\) is nonzero and squares to zero; such an element is nilpotent. Passing to ordinary points removes it. Later scheme theory retains this infinitesimal information; this course's reduced varieties deliberately discard it.

The algebra laboratory verifies the unit-ideal certificate and ideal membership with exact symbolic arithmetic. A symbolic check illustrates these finite examples. The theorem, rather than the experiment, establishes the general dictionary.

1.6 Exercises and complete solutions

  1. Show that the set \(\{(t,t^2,t^3):t\in k\}\subseteq\mathbb A^3\) is algebraic and find its vanishing ideal.
  2. Compute the radical and zero locus of \((x^2,xy,y^3)\subseteq k[x,y]\).
  3. Explain why an infinite subset of \(\mathbb A^1\) is dense and why this does not make it the whole line.
  4. Construct two nonconstant polynomials with no common zero over an algebraically closed field. Give a certificate.
Worked solutions

1. The equations \(y-x^2=0\) and \(z-x^3=0\) force a point to be \((x,x^2,x^3)\), so their zero locus is exactly the given set. The substitution map \(k[x,y,z]\to k[t]\) sends \(x,y,z\) to \(t,t^2,t^3\). Reducing a polynomial modulo \((y-x^2,z-x^3)\) leaves a polynomial \(r(x)\). If the original polynomial vanishes on the set, then \(r(t)=0\) for every \(t\in k\). An algebraically closed field is infinite, so \(r=0\). The two displayed equations generate the full vanishing ideal.

2. Any common zero has \(x=0\) from \(x^2=0\) and \(y=0\) from \(y^3=0\). Both \(x\) and \(y\) lie in the radical. Conversely the original ideal is contained in the maximal ideal \((x,y)\), which is radical, so its radical is contained in \((x,y)\). Thus the radical is \((x,y)\), and the zero locus is the origin.

3. A proper algebraic subset of the line is contained in the roots of one nonzero polynomial and is therefore finite. No proper closed set contains an infinite subset. Its closure is the whole line, although the subset may omit points; the nonzero points already give such an example. Density describes closure, not equality.

4. In one variable use \(x\) and \(x-1\). The identity \(x-(x-1)=1\) is the certificate. Algebraic closure ensures common zeros for proper ideals, not for every collection of nonconstant equations.

Boundary: the weak Nullstellensatz is cited, not reproved from field theory. The strong form, topology identities, finite-generation argument, and examples are developed here. These notes study reduced point-set geometry; they do not claim that point sets retain nonreduced structure.
