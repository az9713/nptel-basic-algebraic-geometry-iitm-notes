A rational expression can look singular in one presentation and be regular in another. The fraction \((x^2-1)/(x-1)\) agrees with \(x+1\) away from 1 and extends regularly across 1. Regularity is therefore a local property of a function, not a test on one displayed denominator.

Lecture spine: L15–16. Quasi-compactness supplies finite local data; regular functions glue that data. The distinction between one global fraction and several local expressions is essential on quasi-affine varieties.

7.1 Quasi-compact does not mean Hausdorff L15 00:00

Definition 7.1. A space is quasi-compact if every open cover has a finite subcover. No Hausdorff assumption is included. An open subset of an affine variety is called quasi-affine.
Proposition 7.2. Every Noetherian space, and every one of its subspaces, is quasi-compact.

For an open cover, consider all finite unions of members of the cover. The ascending-chain condition on open sets implies this collection has a maximal member \(W\). If \(W\ne X\), choose a point outside \(W\) and a covering member containing it. Adding that member gives a strictly larger finite union, a contradiction. Thus \(W=X\). Chapter 3 proved that every subspace of a Noetherian space is Noetherian, so the same argument applies to each subspace. ∎

A direct algebraic proof for affine \(X\) is also informative. Refine an open cover by principal opens \(D(f_\alpha)\). If they cover \(X\), the functions \(f_\alpha\) have no common zero. The Nullstellensatz says their ideal is the whole coordinate ring, so \(1=\sum_{i=1}^r a_if_{\alpha_i}\) for a finite list. No point can make all \(f_{\alpha_i}\) vanish, and those finitely many opens cover. The finite identity is the algebraic content of the finite subcover.

7.2 Regularity is local representability L15 30:00

Definition 7.3. A function \(h:U\to k\), with \(U\) open in an affine variety \(X\), is regular at \(p\in U\) if some open neighborhood \(W\subseteq U\) of \(p\) has an expression \(h=a/b\), with \(a,b\in k[X]\) and \(b\) nonzero at every point of \(W\). It is regular if it is regular at every point. The ring of such functions is denoted \(\mathcal O_X(U)\).

One may instead require \(b(p)\ne0\) and shrink \(W\) to its intersection with \(D(b)\). Sums and products are regular after intersecting their local neighborhoods and multiplying denominators. The constant function 1 is regular, and a regular function nonvanishing at \(p\) has a regular reciprocal near \(p\): invert its local numerator on a smaller neighborhood.

Regular functions are Zariski continuous. Locally write \(h=a/b\); the zero locus of \(h-c\) on that neighborhood is cut out by \(a-cb\). This proves inverse images of points are locally closed in the required sense, and more generally the inverse image of any polynomial zero locus is locally a zero locus after clearing denominators. Closedness is local on an open cover, so the inverse image is closed in \(U\).

Regular functions also glue. If \(U=\bigcup U_i\) and \(h_i\in\mathcal O_X(U_i)\) agree on all overlaps, they define a unique pointwise function \(h\). At any point choose one \(U_i\) and use its local quotient expression; hence \(h\) is regular. These restriction and gluing rules make \(\mathcal O_X\) a sheaf of rings: local functions agreeing where both are defined determine a unique function on their union.

7.3 Every global regular function on an affine is polynomial L16 00:00

Theorem 7.4. If \(X\) is affine and irreducible, with coordinate domain \(A\), then \(\mathcal O_X(X)=A\). For \(f\ne0\), \(\mathcal O_X(D(f))=A_f\).

Let \(h\) be regular on \(X\). Refine its local quotient neighborhoods to principal opens, and use quasi-compactness to select finitely many. On each write \(h=a_i/b_i\), with \(b_i\) nonvanishing there. Every nonempty pair of neighborhoods intersects because \(X\) is irreducible. Agreement as functions gives \(a_ib_j=a_jb_i\) in \(A\): the difference vanishes on a dense nonempty open. Thus the expressions define a single element \(q\in\operatorname{Frac}A\).

Consider the denominator ideal \(J=\{b\in A:bq\in A\}\). It is an ideal. At every point \(p\), one of the local denominators \(b_i\in J\) is nonzero, so \(J\) has no common zero. By the Nullstellensatz \(J=A\). Hence \(1\in J\), and \(q\in A\). Its polynomial function agrees with \(h\) on the covering neighborhoods, proving the first assertion. For the second, Chapter 6 realizes \(D(f)\) as an affine variety with coordinate ring \(A_f\); applying the first assertion to that affine realization proves the result. Regularity is preserved by its explicit coordinate maps. ∎

This proof uses irreducibility to compare all local fractions in one fraction field. There is a corresponding affine theorem for reduced reducible algebraic sets, but its proof must handle componentwise functions rather than assume a single fraction field. We use the domain version needed for this course.

A global regular function on a quasi-affine \(U\) need not be the restriction of a polynomial on the original ambient space. On the punctured line \(D(x)\), the function \(1/x\) is regular but cannot extend to a polynomial on the whole line. Conversely, regularity is stronger than merely possessing a rational expression: \(1/x\) is not regular at the origin of the line.

7.4 A missing point that adds no functions

Let \(U=\mathbb A^2\setminus\{0\}=D(x)\cup D(y)\). By the theorem, a regular function on \(U\) restricts to elements of \(k[x,y]_x\) and \(k[x,y]_y\) agreeing in \(k(x,y)\). Their intersection is \(k[x,y]\). To verify it, write \(q=a/x^r=b/y^s\). Then \(y^sa=x^rb\). Unique factorization and coprimality of \(x,y\) imply \(x^r\mid a\), so \(q\) is polynomial.

Consequently \(\mathcal O(U)=k[x,y]\), even though \(U\ne\mathbb A^2\). A missing codimension-two subset has not created new regular functions in this example. The analogous statement requires hypotheses in general; normality supplies a broader extension principle, but no general theorem of that kind is needed for this calculation.

This example already exposes a limit of the “space equals ring of functions” slogan. It is exact for affine varieties. Without affineness, distinct varieties can have the same ring of global regular functions. Local rings and the full sheaf retain more information than the global ring alone.

7.5 Exercises and complete solutions

  1. Prove that a regular function on the affine line is a polynomial by reducing a rational expression.
  2. For \(D(x)\cup D(x-1)=\mathbb A^1\), write an explicit identity certifying the cover.
  3. Explain why a rational function's denominator vanishing at a point does not alone prove nonregularity.
  4. Show that the inverse of a nowhere-zero regular function is regular.
Worked solutions

1. The preceding proof identifies the function with \(a(t)/b(t)\in k(t)\); cancel common factors. If \(b\) is nonconstant, algebraic closure gives a root \(c\). Coprimality makes \(a(c)\ne0\). Any alternative expression \(u/v\) regular at \(c\) would satisfy \(av=bu\); evaluation gives \(a(c)v(c)=0\), contradicting both factors being nonzero. Thus \(b\) is constant, so the function is polynomial.

2. The identity \(x-(x-1)=1\) shows the two functions have no simultaneous zero. Every point therefore belongs to at least one principal open. It is an algebraic certificate of the cover, not a numerical sample.

3. The expression \((x^2-1)/(x-1)\) has a vanishing denominator at 1 but equals \(x+1\) away from 1 and admits that regular extension. By contrast, the reduced expression \(1/(x-1)\) cannot extend: cross multiplication with a proposed regular expression gives the contradiction used in Exercise 1.

4. Near any point express the function as \(a/b\) with \(b\ne0\). Its value being nonzero implies \(a\ne0\) at that point; shrink to a neighborhood where \(a\) is nonzero. There the inverse is \(b/a\), a permitted expression. These local inverses agree pointwise and therefore glue.
