Affine morphisms, duality, and automorphisms

The punctured affine line is affine, but the punctured affine plane is not. Both are open subsets of affine spaces. The difference becomes visible only when maps and regular functions enter the dictionary: a ring can reconstruct an affine variety, while the same ring of global functions can belong to a different, nonaffine variety.

Lecture spine: L17–21. This chapter develops morphisms, pullbacks, the affine equivalence, polynomial automorphisms, and the punctured-plane example. Unless specified otherwise, \(k\) is algebraically closed and varieties are irreducible. The Jacobian discussion includes a dated research update separate from the 2016 recording.

8.1 Maps must preserve local functions L17 25:00 L18 00:00

A polynomial map between affine spaces pulls polynomial functions backward by substitution. If \(f(x,y)=(x,y+x^2)\) and \(h(u,v)=uv\), then the pulled-back function is \(h(f(x,y))=x(y+x^2)\). This operation goes in the direction opposite to the map of points. On an open subset, regular functions may be fractions rather than polynomials, so the definition must preserve those local expressions as well.

Definition 8.1. A morphism \(f:Y\to X\) is a continuous map for the Zariski topologies such that, for every open \(V\subseteq X\), composition gives a map \[ f^*:\mathcal O_X(V)\longrightarrow\mathcal O_Y(f^{-1}V),\qquad h\longmapsto h\circ f. \] An isomorphism is a morphism having an inverse that is also a morphism. A morphism from a variety to itself is an endomorphism; an isomorphism from it to itself is an automorphism.

The operation \(f^*\) is a \(k\)-algebra homomorphism: composition preserves pointwise sums, products and scalar functions. Identities and compositions of morphisms again satisfy the definition, because inverse images and composition of functions compose. The collection of varieties and morphisms is therefore a category, meaning objects with associative composition of arrows and an identity arrow for each object.

One must inspect functions on all open subsets, not only global functions. Later, every global regular function on an irreducible projective variety will be constant. Preserving those constants alone would place essentially no restriction on a map. The local condition retains the geometry that the global ring can lose.

A bijective morphism need not be an isomorphism. The cusp parametrization \(t\mapsto(t^2,t^3)\) is an example. The set-theoretic inverse exists over \(k\): away from the origin it is \(y/x\), and the origin has the unique preimage zero. Yet its pullback identifies the cusp's coordinate ring with \(k[t^2,t^3]\), a proper subring of \(k[t]\). The missing function \(t\) prevents a regular inverse; the next section makes that implication precise.

8.2 Coordinate functions test a map into an affine L18 10:00 L20 00:00

Proposition 8.2. Let \(X\subseteq\mathbb A^n\) be affine, with coordinate functions \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\). A set map \(f:Y\to X\) is a morphism if and only if the functions \(x_i\circ f\) are regular on \(Y\).

A morphism pulls these regular functions back to regular functions by definition. Conversely, suppose \(f_i=x_i\circ f\) are regular. Every polynomial function \(g\) on \(X\) pulls back to \(g(f_1,\ldots,f_n)\), a polynomial in regular functions and therefore regular. A closed subset of \(X\) is the common zero locus of polynomial functions; its inverse image is the common zero locus of their regular pullbacks, which is closed. Thus \(f\) is continuous.

For a regular function \(h\) on an open \(V\subseteq X\), choose a local expression \(h=a/b\). On its inverse image, \(h\circ f=a(f_1,\ldots,f_n)/b(f_1,\ldots,f_n)\). The denominator is nonzero there. A quotient of regular functions with nowhere-zero denominator is regular, by Chapter 7. These expressions establish the condition on every open \(V\). ∎

In particular, regular functions \(Y\to k\) are exactly morphisms \(Y\to\mathbb A^1\). A tuple of \(n\) regular functions gives a morphism into \(\mathbb A^n\). To land in an affine subvariety \(X\), that tuple must satisfy the defining equations of \(X\) as identities of regular functions.

The proposition also completes the principal-open construction. The map \(D(g)\to V(gz-1)\), \(p\mapsto(p,1/g(p))\), has regular coordinates. Its inverse is the coordinate projection, hence a morphism. This proves an isomorphism, rather than merely a bijection or homeomorphism. It explains why being open in one affine space does not prevent a variety from being closed in a different one.

8.3 All maps into an affine are algebra maps L19 10:00

Theorem 8.3. For any variety \(Y\) and an affine variety \(X\), pullback gives a natural bijection \[ \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathrm{var}}(Y,X) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{k\text{-alg}}\bigl(k[X],\mathcal O_Y(Y)\bigr). \] The left side consists of morphisms of varieties; the right side consists of homomorphisms fixing \(k\).

Write \(k[X]=k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/I(X)\), and denote the classes of the coordinates by \(\bar x_i\). A morphism \(f\) determines \(f^*\) by composition. If two morphisms induce the same homomorphism, their coordinate pullbacks agree, so their values at each point have the same coordinates. The morphisms are equal.

Conversely, given \(\varphi:k[X]\to\mathcal O_Y(Y)\), put \(f_i=\varphi(\bar x_i)\). Define the tuple \(f(y)=(f_1(y),\ldots,f_n(y))\). For \(g\in I(X)\), its class is zero, so \[ g(f_1,\ldots,f_n)=\varphi\bigl(g(\bar x_1,\ldots,\bar x_n)\bigr)=0. \] Thus each tuple lies in \(X\). Proposition 8.2 makes this map a morphism. Its pullback agrees with \(\varphi\) on the generators \(\bar x_i\), hence on the whole coordinate ring. The two constructions are inverse. ∎

When \(Y\) is also affine, Chapter 7 gives \(\mathcal O_Y(Y)=k[Y]\). Every morphism of affine varieties is therefore encoded by exactly one \(k\)-algebra map in the reverse direction. The scheme-theoretic extension of this statement is recorded in Stacks, Section 26.6, “The category of affine schemes”; the proof above uses classical coordinate functions and the affine regular-function theorem.

The assignment \(X\mapsto k[X]\) is contravariant: for \(f:Y\to X\) and \(g:Z\to Y\), \[ (f\circ g)^*=g^*\circ f^*. \] It gives an equivalence between affine varieties and finitely generated \(k\)-algebras that are domains, with arrows reversed. Every algebra on the latter side occurs: choose generators to present it as a polynomial ring modulo a prime ideal, and apply the Nullstellensatz. Reconstructing from another choice of generators gives an isomorphic variety.

Ring isomorphisms consequently give variety isomorphisms. If \(\varphi:k[X]\to k[Y]\) is invertible, the inverse algebra map produces the inverse morphism; composition of pullbacks proves that their point maps compose to identities. This is stronger than recovering the topology of maximal ideals.

The direction of an algebra map also distinguishes different geometric operations. A surjective map \(k[X]\to B\), with \(B\) a domain, describes the closed subvariety defined by its prime kernel; this is a closed immersion. For a morphism \(Y\to X\) between affine varieties, injectivity of the pullback means the image is dense, a property called dominance: the kernel is exactly the functions vanishing on the image and therefore on its closure. An injective pullback does not make the point map injective.

8.4 Polynomial inverses and the Jacobian condition L21 00:00

A morphism \(F:\mathbb A^n\to\mathbb A^n\) is a tuple of polynomials \((F_1,\ldots,F_n)\). Its pullback sends \(x_i\) to \(F_i\). It is an automorphism precisely when this substitution is an automorphism of the polynomial ring. This condition requires a polynomial inverse, not just a set-theoretic inverse or a differentiable inverse near each point.

The triangular map \(F(x,y)=(x,y+x^2)\) has inverse \(G(u,v)=(u,v-u^2)\). Substitution verifies both \(G\circ F=\mathrm{id}\) and \(F\circ G=\mathrm{id}\). Its Jacobian matrix is the matrix of formal partial derivatives, \(J_F=(\partial F_i/\partial x_j)_{i,j}\). Here \[ J_F=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\2x&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det J_F=1. \] Formal differentiation is algebraic: \(\partial x^m/\partial x=mx^{m-1}\), with the integer coefficient interpreted in \(k\).

Proposition 8.4. A polynomial automorphism has nonzero constant Jacobian determinant.

If \(G\) is its polynomial inverse, the formal chain rule gives \(J_G(F(x))J_F(x)=I_n\). Taking determinants gives \((\det J_G)(F(x))\det J_F(x)=1\) in \(k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\). Its units are nonzero constants: total degree of a product of nonzero polynomials is the sum of the degrees. Therefore \(\det J_F\in k^\times\). ∎

In characteristic zero and one variable the converse follows immediately: a polynomial whose derivative is a nonzero constant has degree one. In positive characteristic \(p\), the polynomial \(t-t^p\) has derivative 1, but takes the value zero at every element of the prime subfield \(\mathbb F_p\). It is not injective. The field's characteristic is the least positive integer \(p\) for which \(p\cdot1=0\), or zero if no such integer exists.

Research update, checked 10 November 2026. The recording presents the characteristic-zero converse as the Jacobian conjecture. Gao's November 2026 revised preprint reports explicit counterexamples in dimensions at least three, following Alpöge's announcement. Pissolato's August 2026 preprint likewise describes the higher-dimensional claim as false and the two-dimensional case as open. This is a dated report of those primary sources; the counterexample construction and its proof have not been independently reproduced in this course. The necessary condition proved above remains valid.

The exact algebra laboratory checks the triangular inverse, its Jacobian determinant, and the cusp substitution. These finite checks illustrate the constructions; they do not decide the general converse or certify the reported research counterexamples.

8.5 Why the punctured plane is not affine L21 35:00

Let \(U=\mathbb A^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}\), and let \(i:U\hookrightarrow\mathbb A^2\) be its inclusion. Chapter 7 proved that restriction is an isomorphism \[ i^*:k[x,y]\longrightarrow\mathcal O_U(U). \] For completeness, \(U=D(x)\cup D(y)\), so a regular function belongs to \(k[x,y]_x\cap k[x,y]_y\) inside \(k(x,y)\). If \(a/x^r=b/y^s\), unique factorization in \(k[x,y]\) gives \(x^r\mid a\), hence the fraction is polynomial. Injectivity follows from density of \(U\).

Proposition 8.5. The punctured affine plane is quasi-affine but not affine.

It is a nonempty open subset of the affine plane, hence quasi-affine. Suppose it were affine. The affine equivalence would then apply to \(i\). Since \(i^*\) is an isomorphism, \(i\) would be an isomorphism of varieties and thus a bijection of points. But the origin is not in its image. This contradiction proves that no affine realization of \(U\) exists. ∎

The proof does not claim that \(\mathcal O_U(U)\) is ill-behaved: it is the familiar finitely generated domain \(k[x,y]\). Affineness requires the canonical geometric reconstruction to work, not merely finite generation of the global ring. Removing a point from the line enlarges its functions to \(k[t,t^{-1}]\) and leaves an affine variety; removing the origin from the plane leaves all global functions unchanged and destroys affineness.

This is the boundary of the affine dictionary. A single global ring suffices for affine varieties. For projective and other nonaffine varieties, one must retain rings on open sets and the restriction maps connecting them. The projective charts in Chapter 9 provide the next example of that local-to-global structure.

8.6 Exercises and complete solutions

  1. Determine all automorphisms of \(\mathbb A^1\) over \(k\).
  2. For \(F:\mathbb A^1\to\mathbb A^1\), \(t\mapsto t^2\), describe its pullback and decide whether injectivity of that pullback implies injectivity of \(F\).
  3. Find all morphisms from the punctured line to the hyperbola \(uv=1\).
  4. Show explicitly that the cusp parametrization induces a proper inclusion of coordinate rings.
  5. A quasi-affine variety has a finitely generated domain as its ring of global regular functions. Does this prove it is affine?
Worked solutions

1. A morphism is a polynomial \(f(t)\). If it has a polynomial inverse \(g\), degrees multiply: \(\deg(g\circ f)=\deg g\,\deg f=1\). Thus \(\deg f=1\), giving \(f(t)=at+b\) with \(a\ne0\). Its inverse is \((t-b)/a\), so every such polynomial is an automorphism. This degree argument works in every characteristic.

2. The pullback \(k[u]\to k[t]\) sends \(u\) to \(t^2\), and is injective because substituting \(t^2\) into a nonzero polynomial cannot annihilate its highest-degree term. If \(\operatorname{char}k\ne2\), the points \(a\) and \(-a\) have the same image for \(a\ne0\), so \(F\) is not injective. In characteristic two over algebraically closed \(k\), it is bijective on points, but still has no polynomial inverse by the degree argument. Dominance and isomorphism are distinct properties.

3. A morphism corresponds to an algebra map \(k[u,u^{-1}]\to k[t,t^{-1}]\), so it is determined by a unit, the image of \(u\). Units of the Laurent ring are \(ct^m\), with \(c\in k^\times,m\in\mathbb Z\): the lowest and highest exponents of a product force a unit and its inverse each to have only one exponent. Hence all maps are \(t\mapsto(ct^m,c^{-1}t^{-m})\). This includes constant maps when \(m=0\).

4. Substitute \(x=t^2,y=t^3\) into \(k[x,y]/(y^2-x^3)\). The kernel is zero: reducing modulo the monic polynomial \(y^2-x^3\) leaves \(a(x)+yb(x)\); after substitution the exponents of \(a(t^2)\) are even, whereas those of \(t^3b(t^2)\) are odd. They cannot cancel, so vanishing forces both polynomials to be zero. The image is \(k[t^2,t^3]\). No polynomial in \(t^2,t^3\) has a term of degree one, so \(t\) is not in the image. The map is injective but not surjective.

5. No. The punctured plane is the counterexample just proved: its global regular functions are \(k[x,y]\), a finitely generated domain, yet it is not affine. The missing condition is that the canonical map to the variety reconstructed from the ring be an isomorphism. Global functions alone do not supply that condition.

Boundary: the affine equivalence is proved for the course's classical varieties over algebraically closed \(k\). It is not a claim that every variety is affine or that every bijective morphism is an isomorphism.
