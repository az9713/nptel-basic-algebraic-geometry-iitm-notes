Dimension, coordinate rings, and hypersurfaces

  • 4.1 Dimension counts strict inclusions L8 05:00
  • 4.2 Algebraic independence measures dimension L8 15:00
  • 4.3 Why codimension one means one equation L10 15:00
  • 4.4 Exercises and complete solutions

A plane curve has infinitely many points and an infinite-dimensional space of polynomial functions, but geometric dimension one. Counting points, coordinates in a chosen embedding, or basis vectors of the coordinate ring gives the wrong invariant. Chains of irreducible closed subsets recover the geometric count.

Lecture spine: L8–10. Dimension theorems from commutative algebra are identified as invoked results; their consequences and concrete cases are derived below. This distinction prevents a chain diagram from being mistaken for a proof that all chains have the required lengths.

4.1 Dimension counts strict inclusions L8 05:00

Definition 4.1. The dimension of a nonempty space \(X\) is the supremum of integers \(r\) for which there is a chain \(Z_0\subsetneq Z_1\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq Z_r\) of nonempty irreducible closed subsets. The Krull dimension of a ring \(A\) is the supremum of lengths \(r\) of chains \(P_0\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq P_r\) of prime ideals. Length counts inclusions, not terms.

The affine line has the chain \(\{a\}\subsetneq\mathbb A^1\), of length one. It has no longer chain because its only irreducible closed subsets are points and the whole line. In \(\mathbb A^2\), a point inside a line inside the plane gives a chain of length two. This proves a lower bound, not the upper bound; ruling out more complicated chains requires algebra.

For an algebraic set \(X\), define its coordinate ring as \(k[X]=k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/I(X)\). A residue class is a polynomial function restricted to \(X\): two polynomials represent the same function exactly when their difference vanishes on \(X\). Prime ideals of \(k[X]\) correspond to prime ideals of the polynomial ring containing \(I(X)\). Chapter 2 identifies them with irreducible closed subsets of \(X\), in reverse order. Reversing a finite chain preserves its length, so

\[ \dim X=\dim k[X]. \]

The ring \(k[X]\) is a domain precisely when \(X\) is irreducible. It is reduced, meaning it has no nonzero nilpotents, for every algebraic set under our definition: if a power of a polynomial vanishes pointwise, the polynomial itself does. Reducedness does not exclude zero divisors; the crossing has zero divisors but no nonzero nilpotents.

4.2 Algebraic independence measures dimension L8 15:00

For a domain \(A\), its fraction field \(\operatorname{Frac}(A)\) consists of ratios \(a/b\) with \(b\ne0\), modulo cross multiplication. A field extension \(L/K\) is algebraic if each element of \(L\) is a root of a nonzero one-variable polynomial with coefficients in \(K\). Elements \(u_1,\ldots,u_d\) of an extension of \(k\) are algebraically independent if no nonzero polynomial over \(k\) vanishes on that tuple. A transcendence basis is an algebraically independent tuple over whose generated rational-function field the whole extension is algebraic. Its cardinality is the transcendence degree.

Invoked dimension theorem 4.2. If \(A\) is a finitely generated \(k\)-algebra and a domain, then \[ \dim A=\operatorname{trdeg}_k\operatorname{Frac}(A). \] For a prime \(P\subseteq k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\), \[ \operatorname{ht}P+\dim(k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/P)=n, \] where \(\operatorname{ht}P\) is the supremum of lengths of prime chains ending at \(P\). See Stacks, dimension of finite-type algebras.

The mechanism is Noether normalization: one can choose algebraically independent elements \(u_1,\ldots,u_d\in A\) such that \(A\) is finite as a module over \(k[u_1,\ldots,u_d]\). Each element of \(A\) then satisfies a monic polynomial over that subring; this is called an integral extension. The integral-extension chain theorems preserve dimension, and the fraction fields form an algebraic extension. These are substantial algebraic inputs, not consequences of counting the displayed generators. A proof of normalization is available in Stacks, Section 10.115.

For the parabola, \(k[X]\cong k[t]\), so its fraction field is \(k(t)\), of transcendence degree one. For the cusp \(C=V(y^2-x^3)\), substitution \(x=t^2,y=t^3\) gives \(k[C]=k[t^2,t^3]\). To verify that it introduces no further relation, divide a polynomial by the monic polynomial \(y^2-x^3\), leaving a remainder \(A(x)+yB(x)\). After substitution the remainder is \(A(t^2)+t^3B(t^2)\). The first summand has only even exponents and the second only odd exponents, so they cannot cancel; vanishing forces \(A=B=0\). The substitution kernel is therefore exactly \((y^2-x^3)\). Its image is a domain, so that ideal is prime and is the cusp's full vanishing ideal.

The cusp's fraction field still contains \(t=t^3/t^2\), hence equals \(k(t)\). Both curves have dimension one, although their behavior at the origin differs. Dimension alone cannot detect that singularity.

The dimension formula is a theorem for the stated finite-type setting. In a general ring, concatenating maximal chains does not automatically show that all resulting chains have a common length. We will use the formula under its hypotheses rather than assume that an arbitrary Noetherian ring behaves like a polynomial ring.

4.3 Why codimension one means one equation L10 15:00

For an irreducible closed \(X\subseteq\mathbb A^n\), its codimension is \(n-\dim X\). Call it an irreducible hypersurface when that number is one. The dimension formula gives \(\operatorname{ht}I(X)=1\). A unique factorization domain (UFD) is a domain in which every nonzero nonunit factors into irreducible elements, uniquely up to reordering and multiplication by units. Polynomial rings over a field are UFDs. This property forces a height-one prime ideal to be generated by one irreducible polynomial.

Proposition 4.3. Every height-one prime in a UFD is principal.

Choose \(0\ne a\in P\), and factor it into irreducibles. Primality implies that one irreducible factor \(f\) lies in \(P\). In a UFD an irreducible is prime, so \((f)\) is a prime ideal. We have \((0)\subsetneq(f)\subseteq P\). If the last inclusion were strict, it would give a chain of length two ending at \(P\), contrary to height one. Thus \(P=(f)\). ∎

Conversely, let \(f\) be a nonconstant irreducible polynomial. Its ideal is prime in this UFD. The principal ideal theorem, invoked here, states that a prime minimal over a principal ideal in a Noetherian ring has height at most one. Since \((f)\ne(0)\) in a domain, its height is exactly one. The dimension formula gives \(\dim V(f)=n-1\). For a reducible nonconstant polynomial, factorization shows that every irreducible component of its zero locus is still a hypersurface.

The phrase “one equation cuts dimension by one” needs qualifications. The zero polynomial cuts nothing, a nonzero constant cuts everything away, and adding an equation already implied by earlier equations changes nothing. For instance, \(x=0\) and \(xy=0\) together define the same plane as \(x=0\) in three-space. Counting the number of equations is only a heuristic until independence or a dimension theorem justifies it.

There is another subtlety: saying a codimension-one subset is the zero set of one function is weaker than saying its prime ideal is generated by that function. Powers may have the same zero set. The UFD criterion concerns principal prime ideals, not just set-theoretically principal zero loci.

4.4 Exercises and complete solutions

  1. Compute the dimensions of \(V(xy)\subseteq\mathbb A^2\) and \(V(x,y)\subseteq\mathbb A^3\).
  2. Show that \(V(z-xy)\subseteq\mathbb A^3\) has dimension two, without a chain-length guess.
  3. Why is \(k[t]\) infinite-dimensional as a vector space but Krull dimension one?
  4. Give an algebraic set whose irreducible components have different dimensions.
Worked solutions

1. The crossing is the union of two lines, so its dimension is one. An irreducible chain in a finite union lies inside a component, which shows that the dimension of a finite union of closed sets is the maximum of their dimensions. The second set is the \(z\)-axis and has coordinate ring \(k[z]\), hence dimension one. Its codimension in three-space is two.

2. Reduction modulo \(z-xy\) leaves an arbitrary polynomial in \(x,y\). The quotient ring is \(k[x,y]\), whose fraction field has transcendence degree two. The dimension theorem therefore gives two. The graph is an affine plane with a different embedding.

3. The powers \(1,t,t^2,\ldots\) are linearly independent over \(k\), so there is no finite vector-space basis. Prime chains in \(k[t]\) have the form \((0)\subsetneq(t-a)\), of length one. Vector-space dimension counts linear generators; Krull dimension counts prime inclusions. They solve different problems.

4. Use \(V(xy,xz)\subseteq\mathbb A^3\). Chapter 3 decomposed it into the plane \(x=0\) and the line \(y=z=0\). Their dimensions are two and one; the total set has dimension two. It is not equidimensional, a term meaning that all irreducible components have the same dimension.

Boundary: normalization, the finite-type dimension theorem, unique factorization for polynomial rings, and the principal ideal theorem are explicit external algebraic inputs. The notes derive their geometric consequences and do not claim to prove those full theorems from scratch.
