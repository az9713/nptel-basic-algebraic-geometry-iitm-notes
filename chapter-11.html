Germs and local rings

A rational expression can fail to exist globally and still behave perfectly near a chosen point. The function \(1/(1-x)\) is regular near \(x=0\), and its first-order behavior is \(1+x\). A local ring records every algebraic function available near one point, while quotienting powers of its maximal ideal separates constant, linear, and higher-order information.

Lecture spine: official English captions of L28–30, read in order, from finite affine covers through germs and local-ring formulas. The derivations and examples below are original exposition. The default field \(k\) is algebraically closed, varieties are irreducible and reduced, and all morphisms are over \(k\). The first-order examples are teaching extensions. No complete visual or audio review is claimed.

11.1 Why every local calculation can use an affine chart L28 00:00

A projective variety is covered by its intersections with the standard affine charts of projective space. Those intersections are closed in the charts and are therefore affine varieties when nonempty. A quasi-projective variety is open in such a projective variety; intersecting it with these affine pieces gives quasi-affine open subsets. It remains to express each of those open subsets as a union of affine ones.

Let \(U\) be open in an affine variety \(Y\), and let its complement be \(V_Y(J)\), where \(J=(g_1,\ldots,g_r)\subseteq k[Y]\). Such a finite generating list exists because \(k[Y]\) is Noetherian. Then \[ U=\bigcup_{i=1}^r D_Y(g_i). \] Each nonempty \(D_Y(g_i)\) is affine by its graph realization \(g_i z=1\), with coordinate ring \(k[Y]_{g_i}\). Thus every variety in this course has a finite cover by open affine varieties. The empty members of the cover are discarded.

Consequently, a point \(p\) in an arbitrary variety has an affine open neighborhood. Any construction depending only on sufficiently small open neighborhoods can be computed in that affine neighborhood. This observation does not say the whole variety is affine: the projective line and the punctured plane are counterexamples. It says that affine coordinate rings supply the local calculations even when global geometry needs gluing.

11.2 A function near a point L28 30:00

Fix a variety \(X\) and a point \(p\in X\). A regular function defined near \(p\) is a pair \((U,f)\), where \(U\) is an open neighborhood of \(p\) and \(f\in\mathcal O_X(U)\). The choice of neighborhood should not be part of its local identity. Two functions defined on different neighborhoods can describe exactly the same behavior after both are restricted to a smaller neighborhood.

Definition 11.1. Two pairs \((U,f)\) and \((V,g)\) have the same germ at \(p\) if there is an open neighborhood \(W\subseteq U\cap V\) of \(p\) on which \(f=g\). The set of germs, with addition and multiplication defined on common neighborhoods, is the local ring \(\mathcal O_{X,p}\).

Applied to the sheaf \(\mathcal O_X\), this construction is its stalk at \(p\). The general equivalence-class definition is recorded in Stacks, Section 6.11. Here we work directly with functions so the relation and operations can be checked without an abstract colimit construction.

This is an equivalence relation. Reflexivity and symmetry are immediate. For transitivity, intersect the two neighborhoods witnessing equality. Addition and multiplication are independent of representatives because equal functions remain equal after further restriction. The word germ means an equivalence class, not a formal power series and not merely the value \(f(p)\).

For the affine line near zero, the functions \(1/(1-x)\), \(1+x+x^2/(1-x)\), and their restrictions to any smaller neighborhood avoiding 1 define the same germ. Their common value is 1, but they are not the same germ as the constant function 1: their difference \(x/(1-x)\) does not vanish on any nonempty open subset. Equality of values discards variation that the germ retains.

Because a nonempty open subset of an irreducible variety is dense, equality of two regular functions on one such subset implies equality wherever both are defined. A germ can therefore be regarded as a particular rational function that is regular at the chosen point. Chapter 12 formalizes rational functions independently of the point.

11.3 Why this ring has one maximal ideal L28 45:00

Evaluating a representative at \(p\) gives a well-defined surjective homomorphism \(\mathcal O_{X,p}\to k\), since two representatives of one germ agree on a neighborhood containing \(p\). Its kernel is denoted \(\mathfrak m_p\); these are precisely the germs vanishing at \(p\). The quotient \(\mathcal O_{X,p}/\mathfrak m_p\cong k\) is called the residue field.

Proposition 11.2. A germ in \(\mathcal O_{X,p}\) is invertible if and only if its value at \(p\) is nonzero. Consequently, \(\mathfrak m_p\) is the unique maximal ideal of \(\mathcal O_{X,p}\).

If a germ has a multiplicative inverse, evaluation sends their product to 1, so neither value is zero. Conversely, a representative \(f\) with \(f(p)\ne0\) is nonvanishing on an open neighborhood of \(p\), because regular functions are continuous. Its reciprocal is regular there by the local quotient definition. Thus every element outside \(\mathfrak m_p\) is a unit. Any proper ideal contains no unit and is therefore contained in \(\mathfrak m_p\). Since the quotient by \(\mathfrak m_p\) is the field \(k\), this ideal is maximal and no other maximal ideal exists. ∎

A ring with a unique maximal ideal is called a local ring. This algebraic definition explains the geometric terminology. All functions that do not vanish at the point are available as invertible denominators; only the functions vanishing there remain possible nonunits.

One must distinguish three conditions. A function may be nonzero as an element of a domain, may be nonzero at \(p\), or may be nowhere zero on all of \(X\). The coordinate \(x\) on the line is a nonzero polynomial but vanishes at zero. The polynomial \(1-x\) is nonzero at zero and is a unit in \(\mathcal O_{\mathbb A^1,0}\), though it is not a unit in the global coordinate ring \(k[x]\).

11.4 The affine localization formula L29 35:00

Let \(X\subseteq\mathbb A^n\) be affine, with coordinate domain \(A=k[X]\). The point \(p\) determines the maximal ideal \(\mathfrak m_p=\{a\in A:a(p)=0\}\) in \(A\). Context distinguishes it from its extension to the local ring. The localization \(A_{\mathfrak m_p}\) allows fractions \(a/b\) with \(b\notin\mathfrak m_p\), equivalently \(b(p)\ne0\).

Theorem 11.3. Restriction to a germ induces a natural \(k\)-algebra isomorphism \[ \mathcal O_{X,p}\cong A_{\mathfrak m_p}. \]

A fraction \(a/b\), with \(b(p)\ne0\), is regular on \(D_X(b)\), a neighborhood of \(p\), so it defines a germ. Every germ has such a local quotient representation by the definition of regularity, giving surjectivity. If \(a/b\) and \(c/d\) determine the same germ, then \(ad-bc\) vanishes on a nonempty open neighborhood. That neighborhood is dense in the irreducible \(X\), so \(ad-bc=0\) in \(A\). This is equality of the fractions in the fraction field. Hence the map is injective. It respects addition, multiplication and scalars because all are defined by the same pointwise operations. ∎

Writing \(X=V(P)\), where \(P\subseteq k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\) is prime, gives an equivalent formula. If \(M_p=(x_1-p_1,\ldots,x_n-p_n)\), then \[ \mathcal O_{X,p}\cong \bigl(k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/P\bigr)_{M_p/P} \cong k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]_{M_p}/P\,k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]_{M_p}. \] The second isomorphism follows by sending a fraction modulo \(P\) to the class of the corresponding localized fraction. Its denominators have nonzero value at \(p\), so they remain outside the relevant maximal ideal. Both directions are inverse on polynomial classes and permitted reciprocals.

Any open neighborhood \(U\) of \(p\) has the same local ring as \(X\) at \(p\). A germ on \(X\) restricts to \(U\); a germ on \(U\) is already defined on an open neighborhood in \(X\), since \(U\) itself is open. The two constructions are inverse. Removing points elsewhere does not affect the functions available sufficiently near \(p\).

For a morphism \(f:X\to Y\) with \(f(p)=q\), pullback induces a homomorphism \(\mathcal O_{Y,q}\to\mathcal O_{X,p}\): a representative on \(V\) pulls back to one on \(f^{-1}(V)\), which contains \(p\). This respects the germ relation and has inverse image of \(\mathfrak m_p\) equal to \(\mathfrak m_q\), because evaluation commutes with pullback. Such a homomorphism is called local. If \(f\) is an isomorphism, pulling back along its inverse gives the inverse local-ring map. The local ring is therefore an invariant of a variety with its chosen point, up to \(k\)-algebra isomorphism; it does not by itself assert a global isomorphism of varieties.

11.5 The projective local-ring formula L30 00:00

Let \(X\subseteq\mathbb P^n\) be a projective variety with homogeneous coordinate domain \(S=k[x_0,\ldots,x_n]/I(X)\). For a homogeneous element \(f\) of positive degree, the ring \(S_{(f)}\) is the degree-zero part of the localization \(S_f\). In particular, on the chart \(U_i=X\cap D_+(x_i)\), its elements have representatives \(g/x_i^r\), where \(g\) is homogeneous of degree \(r\). These are genuine scalar-valued functions on projective points because numerator and denominator scale by the same power.

Choose a point \(p=[a_0:\cdots:a_n]\) with \(a_i\ne0\). The chart ring \(B_i=S_{(x_i)}\) is affine. Let \(\mathfrak n_{p,i}\subseteq B_i\) be the kernel of evaluation at \(p\). The affine formula and neighborhood invariance give \[ \mathcal O_{X,p}\cong (B_i)_{\mathfrak n_{p,i}}. \] Thus the projective formula is not a new type of local object. One dehomogenizes on any chart containing the point, then localizes at that point.

There is a chart-independent version. Let \(\mathfrak p_p\subseteq S\) be the homogeneous prime ideal of all homogeneous forms vanishing at \(p\). Homogeneous localization at \(p\), denoted \(S_{(\mathfrak p_p)}\), consists of degree-zero fractions \(g/h\), with \(g,h\) homogeneous of the same degree and \(h(p)\ne0\). Then \[ \mathcal O_{X,p}\cong S_{(\mathfrak p_p)}. \] To compare with a chart, divide numerator and denominator by the same power of \(x_i\). The denominator becomes an element of \(B_i\) nonzero at \(p\), hence invertible after localization. Conversely, any fraction from the localized chart can be expressed with equal-degree homogeneous numerator and denominator after clearing powers of \(x_i\).

On \(\mathbb P^1\), the point at infinity \([0:1]\) belongs to the chart \(x_1\ne0\), with coordinate \(s=x_0/x_1\). Its local ring is \(k[s]_{(s)}\). The function \(x_1/x_0=1/s\) is not regular there, while \(x_0/x_1=s\) is. The choice of projective coordinates changes the formulas, but not the intrinsic local ring of the pointed variety.

The notation \(\mathfrak p_p\) in the graded ring must not be confused with an ordinary maximal ideal of that ring. For the point \([0:1]\), the homogeneous ideal is \((x_0)\subseteq k[x_0,x_1]\); its quotient is \(k[x_1]\), not a field. The point becomes maximal only in its affine chart ring or in its local ring.

For coordinates \(p=[a_0:\cdots:a_n]\), its homogeneous ideal in the polynomial ring is generated by the linear forms \(a_i x_j-a_jx_i\). On a chart with \(a_i\ne0\), these become the affine point equations \(x_j/x_i-a_j/a_i\). Indeed their common affine-cone solutions are the scalar multiples of the vector \((a_0,\ldots,a_n)\). This verifies directly why the homogeneous point ideal and the affine-chart maximal ideal produce the same local ring. The degree-zero localization conventions agree with Stacks, Section 27.8.

11.6 First-order information without limits

Let \(R=\mathcal O_{X,p}\), with maximal ideal \(\mathfrak m\). The quotient \(R/\mathfrak m\) retains only the value at \(p\). The quotient \(R/\mathfrak m^2\) retains that value and first-order variation. The vector space \(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2\) over \(k\) is the cotangent space; multiplication by a local function depends there only on its value, because multiplying an element of \(\mathfrak m\) by another element of \(\mathfrak m\) vanishes in the quotient.

For \(R=k[x]_{(x)}\), every germ has the first-order form \(a+bx\) modulo \(x^2\). For example, \[ \frac1{1-x}-(1+x)=\frac{x^2}{1-x}\in(x^2)R. \] Hence the two germs agree to first order but are not equal. More generally, for \(r\ge0\), \[ \frac1{1-x}-\sum_{j=0}^{r}x^j=\frac{x^{r+1}}{1-x}. \] This is an exact finite algebraic identity, valid in the local ring. It needs no analytic convergence or absolute value on \(k\).

The parabola \(y=x^2\) at the origin has local ring \(k[x]_{(x)}\), with maximal ideal generated by \(x\); \(y\) lies in its square, so it contributes no independent first-order direction. At the cusp \(y^2=x^3\), the origin has maximal ideal generated by \(x,y\). Its defining relation has no linear term, so it imposes no linear relation on \(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2\). The cotangent space has dimension two although the curve has dimension one. Chapter 15 uses this mismatch to detect nonsingularity.

The local ring is smaller than its formal power-series completion. For instance, \(k[x]_{(x)}\) consists of rational functions regular at zero; arbitrary formal coefficient sequences need not be rational. Completion is useful for infinite formal expansions, but finite quotients \(R/\mathfrak m^{r+1}\) already support exact finite-order calculations.

11.7 Exercises and complete solutions

  1. At \(p=2\) on the affine line, determine whether \(1/x\), \(1/(x-2)\), and \((x^2-4)/(x-2)\) define regular germs. Interpret the last expression correctly.
  2. Compute the local ring at \([1:0]\in\mathbb P^1\), and explain why the two chart descriptions at overlapping points agree.
  3. Show that a ring homomorphism induced by a morphism at a point is local: it sends the target maximal ideal into the source maximal ideal.
  4. Compute the classes of \(1/(1-x)\) modulo \((x^3)\) and \(1/(1+x)\) modulo \((x^3)\) in \(k[x]_{(x)}\).
  5. For \(X=V(y-x^2)\), prove directly that the cotangent space at the origin is one-dimensional.
Worked solutions

1. The first denominator is nonzero at 2 provided \(2\ne0\) in \(k\); under that hypothesis \(1/x\) is regular there. The second has a genuine pole: if \(1/(x-2)=a/b\) with \(b(2)\ne0\), then \(b=(x-2)a\), contradicting evaluation at 2. The third expression equals \(x+2\) away from 2 and therefore has the regular extension \(x+2\) at 2. As a literal quotient it is undefined at 2; its rational-function class admits a regular germ. In characteristic two, \(2=0\), so the first conclusion changes and the same localization test must be applied at zero.

2. Use the chart \(x_0\ne0\), with \(t=x_1/x_0\); the point has \(t=0\), so the local ring is \(k[t]_{(t)}\). At a point lying in both charts, \(t\ne0\) and \(s=1/t\). The transition is a regular isomorphism on the overlap, and restriction to the point gives inverse local-ring maps. A different chart changes a generator, not the germ equivalence classes.

3. For a morphism \(f:X\to Y\) with \(f(p)=q\), pullback sends a regular function near \(q\) to one near \(p\). If the germ vanishes at \(q\), its pullback has value zero at \(p\), so it belongs to \(\mathfrak m_p\). More precisely, the inverse image of \(\mathfrak m_p\) is \(\mathfrak m_q\), because a nonzero value remains the same nonzero scalar after pullback. This is the local-homomorphism condition.

4. The exact identities give \(1/(1-x)\equiv1+x+x^2\pmod{x^3}\) and \(1/(1+x)\equiv1-x+x^2\pmod{x^3}\). Multiply each proposed polynomial by its denominator; the difference from 1 is divisible by \(x^3\). The calculation remains valid in any characteristic, with signs interpreted in the field.

5. In the coordinate ring, \(y=x^2\), so the local ring is \(k[x]_{(x)}\). Its maximal ideal is \((x)\), and its square is \((x^2)\). Every element of \((x)\) is \(x\) times a germ; modulo \((x^2)\), that coefficient can be replaced by its value at zero. Thus the class of \(x\) spans. It is nonzero, since \(x=x^2a/b\) with \(b(0)\ne0\) would imply \(b=xa\), contradicting \(b(0)\ne0\). The cotangent space therefore has basis \([x]\).

Boundary: local rings retain regular rational behavior near a point. They are not merely point values, arbitrary formal series, or a claim that a sufficiently small analytic ball has been chosen. The cotangent construction is algebraic; analytic manifold conclusions require the later field and nonsingularity hypotheses.
